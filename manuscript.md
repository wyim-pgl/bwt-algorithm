Abstract

**Motivation:** Tandem repeat periods span mononucleotide microsatellites to satellite monomers of one to two kilobases, and none of the widely used detectors covers that span cheaply. They take a maximum period, and what a wide one costs is tool-specific and not predictable from the tool's description: on the maize genome ULTRA takes 0.23 hours at a maximum period of 6 bp and 34.7 hours at 500 bp, while TRF over the same span is flat at 5.2 and 5.5 hours. A user searching for an uncharacterised satellite must nonetheless bound its monomer length in advance — the quantity a de novo scan is meant to discover — and, not knowing which case they are in, must bound it conservatively; we capped our own TRF runs at 500 bp on human and 200 bp on Arabidopsis for that reason.

**Results:** We present BWTandem, which seeds candidate repeats from FM-index queries for sparsely sampled k-mers and aligns only confirmed candidates, making detection cost largely independent of the period range requested. Widening its own search twentyfold, from 1–100 bp to 1–2,000 bp on human GRCh38, at a fixed thread count, costs 1.40 times the runtime and no additional memory, where the one per-period scanner we measured the same way paid more than linearly — an 83-fold range increase for a 151-fold cost increase. Against the competitors it searched 1–2,000 bp in 12.1 hours while ULTRA took 29.8 hours restricted to 1–100 bp and TRF 33.7 hours at a 500 bp maximum. Against the GIAB adotto benchmark, restricted to the period range every tool could search, it recovers 79.9% of catalogued regions to ULTRA's 81.6%. Above period 100, where ULTRA and tantan, run at their default 100 bp ceilings, report nothing, it reaches 3.4% of the catalog against TRF's 2.0%. Sensitivity is a run-time choice: four configurations trace an operating point range from 54.7% region recall at 62.2% precision — the highest precision of any method here recovering more than half the benchmark, and the point at which the optional periodicity pass is off — to 81.6% recall at 48.4%; the 79.9% above is the third of the four. On the Arabidopsis Col-CEN centromeres it recovers 99.7% of CEN180 monomers in 31 minutes, matching NCRF, which was given the consensus, TRASH, which was not, and — once given a period range that can contain the 178 bp monomer — ULTRA at 99.8% in 10.1 hours and TRF at 97.6% in 131.9 hours. On maize it gives the highest CentC array coverage of any tool tested, though by 0.35 percentage points over the runner-up and without an uncertainty estimate, and at the cost of calls fragmented into hundreds of intervals per array — a fragmentation shared by every de novo tool except TRASH, so an analysis needing one interval per array must merge. Its memory footprint is the largest of the de novo tools compared and grows with thread count, at about 8 GB per thread over a fixed base of roughly 5 GB on this genome.

**Availability and implementation:** Source code, benchmark and scoring scripts, the operating-point figure and its underlying values, and a provenance manifest recording for every accuracy figure its source interval file, scoring script and hash, scoring rule and repository commit are available at https://github.com/wyim-pgl/bwt-algorithm. The manifest carries runtime and memory for the BWTandem runs from the job accounting records; the competitor cost figures are inherited from earlier executions for which no job record survives and are marked as such. The runs reported here were executed from a working tree that was not committed at the time; that tree is now commit 294f8ac, which the manifest records against every row. It differs from the executed code in one respect, described in Section 2.2.1: it carries the fix that stops the satellite gap-filling pass from emitting motifs containing characters other than A, C, G and T, so re-running the released code on the human genome will not reproduce the 198 assembly-gap calls present in the deposited output.

1. Introduction
Tandem repeats—contiguous copies of a DNA sequence motif arranged head-to-tail—are ubiquitous in eukaryotic genomes. They range from microsatellites with 1–6 bp repeat units to megabase-scale satellite arrays that define centromeric and pericentromeric chromatin (Benson, 1999; Naish et al., 2021). Tandem repeats contribute to genome instability through replication slippage and unequal crossing over, are implicated in dozens of heritable human diseases through pathogenic repeat expansions, and serve as substrates for kinetochore assembly in centromeres (Willard, 1998; Mirkin, 2007). Their accurate detection matters for clinical diagnosis, forensic profiling, structural variant calling, and assembly quality control. They also remain among the most difficult genomic elements to characterize computationally.

The difficulty is that short tandem repeats (STRs, periods 1–6 bp) and satellite DNA (periods 100–2,000+ bp) present fundamentally different detection problems. STRs require identification of near-perfect microsatellite runs, whereas satellites tolerate substantial inter-copy divergence — 20–30% within a CEN180 array (Naish et al., 2021) — and span megabases. Tandem Repeats Finder (TRF; Benson, 1999), the de facto standard for over two decades, uses k-tuple matching followed by wraparound dynamic programming and handles both classes reasonably well, but imposes a maximum detectable period of 2,000 bp and can become prohibitively slow on dense satellite arrays where candidate alignments proliferate combinatorially (Wlodzimierz et al., 2023). In practice the binding constraint is cost rather than that ceiling. Every period-parameterised detector must be told its search range in advance, and what a wide one costs is tool-specific and not predictable from the tool's description: on the maize genome ULTRA took 0.23 h at a maximum period of 6 bp, 12.9 h at 200 bp, and 34.7 h at 500 bp, while TRF over that same span was flat, at 5.2 h and 5.5 h. Because a user cannot know in advance which case they are in, the safe choice is a narrow range; our own TRF runs were capped at 500 bp on human and 200 bp on Arabidopsis for that reason. A method whose cost is insensitive to the requested range removes the choice and can search the whole span in one pass, which is the design goal pursued here.

Subsequent tools have attacked different parts of the problem. Alignment-free and combinatorial methods such as mreps (Kolpakov et al., 2003) using Hamming distance and tantan (Frith, 2011) using low-complexity masking are fast but limited. mreps does not model indels, and tantan identifies regions for masking rather than characterizing individual repeat units. Probabilistic approaches such as ULTRA (Olson and Wheeler, 2024) apply HMM-based Viterbi decoding to label sequence as repetitive and report a period and consensus for each labelled region. At the other end, template-guided tools like NCRF (Harris et al., 2019) and satellite-specialized annotators like TRASH (Wlodzimierz et al., 2023) achieve high accuracy for known repeat families, but NCRF requires motifs to be specified a priori and TRASH requires substantial runtime on large genomes. TideHunter (Gao et al., 2019) was designed for noisy long reads rather than assembled genomes.

The completion of telomere-to-telomere (T2T) assemblies for human (Nurk et al., 2022), Arabidopsis thaliana (Naish et al., 2021), and Zea mays (Chen et al., 2023) has resolved centromeric and satellite-rich regions for the first time. Combined with the GIAB consortium's adotto tandem repeat benchmark for human (English et al., 2024), these resources make it possible to evaluate detection tools across the full range of repeat classes on complete genome sequences.

Compressed indexes are not new to repeat analysis; suffix arrays and the FM-index underpin read mappers and have been used to enumerate exact repeats. To our knowledge, what has not been available is a detector whose cost is insensitive to the period range it is asked to search. TRF, ULTRA, and mreps all take a maximum period, which forces the user to bound the arrays they are trying to find before finding them, and for ULTRA that bound is expensive to set generously; tantan takes the same kind of argument and defaults to a maximum period of 100 bp, which we left at its default, so it reports nothing above that here; and the template-guided tools sidestep the question by requiring the motif itself.

Here we present BWTandem, which uses the FM-index to replace exhaustive position-by-position alignment with targeted queries that return the suffix-array interval of a sampled k-mer in time proportional to the k-mer length, and its occurrence positions in time proportional to their number. Sparsely sampled k-mers whose occurrence positions form periodic patterns identify candidate repeat regions directly, and alignment runs only on confirmed candidates, avoiding the combinatorial cost that alignment-based methods incur on dense satellite arrays. BWTandem implements a three-tier pipeline: direct character comparison for microsatellites, optionally supplemented by an FM-index enumeration pass over short motifs — the configuration used for every run reported here — then LCP array analysis with BWT k-mer seeding for medium-length repeats, and sparse BWT k-mer seeding with adaptive parameters for long satellite arrays.

Three properties follow from that design and are what this paper demonstrates. First, a single run covers periods of 1 to 2,000 bp on the human genome in 12.1 hours of wall clock, against 29.8 hours for ULTRA restricted to 1–100 bp and 33.7 hours for TRF at a 500 bp maximum: a twentyfold wider search than ULTRA's at 41% of its wall clock. BWTandem and ULTRA ran on two threads and TRF on one, so in core-hours the figures are 24.2, 59.6 and 33.7, and the margin over TRF is narrower than the wall clock suggests. Second, detection requires no prior knowledge of the repeat families present, which on the Arabidopsis centromere is the difference between recovering 99.7% of CEN180 monomers and recovering 0.6% — the figure the closest competitor returns at its default period ceiling of 100 bp, which cannot represent a 178 bp monomer. That competitor needed only a higher ceiling, not a target length, and Section 3.2 reports what it recovers once given one. Third, sensitivity is set at run time rather than fixed, so one build of the pipeline serves an operating-point curve spanning 54.7% to 81.6% region recall, and a user chooses where on it to sit. We benchmarked on three genomes spanning a twenty-threefold range in size against seven existing tools, six of which produced results we report; TideHunter was excluded for runtime without a completed run.
2. Methods
2.1 BWTandem Algorithm Architecture
2.1.1 Indexing and Data Structures
Given a genomic sequence S of length n, BWTandem constructs a suffix array using divsufsort (Mori, 2008), which is O(n log n) in the worst case and near-linear in practice on genomic input, and derives the Burrows-Wheeler Transform in a single linear pass. An FM-index (Ferragina and Manzini, 2000) is built on these structures. Backward search returns the suffix-array interval of any query of length k in O(k) time, independent of how many times the query occurs; recovering the occ occurrence positions within that interval costs a further O(occ). It is the first of these that the seeding strategy below exploits, since a candidate period can be read from the occurrence list without any scan of the sequence. For medium-length repeat detection, the longest common prefix (LCP) array is simultaneously computed in O(n) time (Kasai et al., 2001). Index construction requires O(n) space.

BWTandem processes each chromosome independently, constructing a single FM-index and then applying a three-tier detection pipeline in sequence. Each tier records detected regions in a coverage mask so that subsequent tiers skip previously identified intervals.
2.1.2 Tier 1: Short Tandem Repeat Detection (Periods 1–9 bp)
The first tier detects microsatellites with periods of one to nine base pairs through direct character comparison, without using the FM-index. For each candidate period p ∈ {1, …, 9}, a boolean match vector M is constructed where M[i] = 1 iff S[i] = S[i + p]. Consecutive runs of ones identify candidate repeat regions, retained only if the array is at least as long as both a period-dependent copy-count minimum, which imposes stricter thresholds on shorter periods, and an absolute length floor. Under the settings used here the length floor is the binding constraint for periods of five and below (Supplementary Methods S1.1).

Candidates are bidirectionally extended while the cumulative Hamming mismatch fraction remains below 20%, then refined via banded dynamic programming alignment, which simultaneously reduces the motif to its primitive period and computes consensus statistics. Candidates with quality scores below a minimum threshold are discarded.

An optional FM-index enumeration mode, enabled for every run reported in this paper, runs as an additive second stage over the sequence the sliding-window scan did not already claim. It queries every primitive motif rotation of each enumerated period length — 1 to 6 bp in this configuration, since rotations are not collapsed to a canonical form — to retrieve their genomic positions. Positions forming approximate arithmetic progressions are grouped into candidate arrays, and a log-likelihood ratio test comparing observed periodic hit density against an independent-bases null with empirically estimated base composition suppresses false positives. The pass applies its own acceptance gate rather than the copy-count minimum above (Supplementary Methods S1.1).
2.1.3 Tier 2: Medium-Length Imperfect Repeat Detection (Periods ≥ 10 bp)
The second tier targets minisatellites and medium-length repeats containing substitutions and indels through two complementary phases.

In the first phase, the LCP array is scanned to identify adjacent suffixes sharing long common prefixes. For each pair where the LCP value exceeds a threshold, the positional difference d = |SA[i] − SA[i + 1]| is computed. If d falls within the target period range, it serves as a candidate period seed. Seeds are extended bidirectionally with up to 30% mismatch tolerance in the runs reported here — the library default is 20%, and every run in this paper raised it (Supplementary Methods S2) — reduced to their primitive period via divisibility checks and autocorrelation, and refined through banded dynamic programming alignment.

The second phase applies BWT k-mer seeding to uncovered regions, which is the primary mechanism through which the FM-index drives repeat detection. K-mers are sampled at configurable intervals and queried via backward search in O(k) time. Occurrence positions are retrieved, sorted, and tested for arithmetic progressions with common difference within the target period range (3% jitter tolerance). Progressions with sufficient copies define candidate regions, which are extended bidirectionally and refined as in the first phase. The two phases cover different period windows: the seeding phase is applied to periods of 10–50 bp and the strict LCP phase to periods of 20 bp upward, so periods of 51–99 bp are reached through exact long-common-prefix seeds alone, and periods of 10–19 bp through k-mer seeding alone.

For highly diverged sequences where exact k-mer recurrence fails, an autocorrelation-based mode computes the fraction of positions where S[i] = S[i + p] across sliding windows. Contiguous stretches exceeding a minimum identity threshold (default 78%) are flagged as periodic, recovering arrays too diverged for seed-based detection. This mode is opt-in and was left disabled in every run reported here; on the human benchmark the arrays it recovers lie inside regions the other phases already touch, so it moves region recall little while lowering base pair precision.
2.1.4 Tier 3: Long Satellite Repeat Detection (Periods 100 bp to 100 kbp)
The third tier detects long satellite arrays and centromeric repeats using the same BWT k-mer seeding procedure as Tier 2, with sparser sampling and longer k-mers suited to large period scales.

The tier automatically adapts its parameters based on input sequence properties. K-mer size scales logarithmically with sequence length (k = 10 + 6 · log₁₀(max(n / 10⁵, 1))), sampling stride scales proportionally to n / 40,000, and the maximum allowed k-mer occurrence count is set to n / 30,000 to filter low-complexity k-mers. GC content modulates the mismatch tolerance. When prior tier coverage exceeds 50%, the stride is reduced to scan uncovered regions more densely. All adaptive parameters are then clamped to empirically validated ranges, and sequences above 100 Mb take additional fixed caps (Supplementary Methods S1.3). At chromosome scale most of these formulas saturate against their bounds, so the values actually in force across all three genomes are a k-mer size of 28, a stride of 300, a maximum occurrence count of 500, and a mismatch tolerance of 15.3–16.4%; the coverage-dependent stride reduction has no effect on sequences above roughly 24 Mb, because the clamp binds before it does. The formulas are given here because they determine behaviour on shorter sequences, not because they set the values used in this paper.

Boundary refinement uses two strategies depending on array scale. For large arrays (more than 10 kb or more than 100 copies), an anchor-based strategy scans forward and backward in period-length increments, terminating when the match fraction drops below a threshold. This linear-time approach avoids the quadratic dynamic programming costs that megabase arrays would otherwise incur. For smaller arrays, banded Needleman-Wunsch alignment determines precise boundaries.
2.1.5 Post-Processing and Supplementary Detection Modes
Post-processing consolidates raw detections by sorting coordinates, merging adjacent calls of the same motif family, and resolving overlaps. Three constants govern this, and we state them because they act directly on the coverage and base pair metrics reported below. First, the merge gap is period-dependent: max(10, p) bp for periods below 20, 10p for periods of 20–99, and 100p for periods of 100 and above, so two calls of a 178 bp monomer separated by as much as 17.8 kb of unclaimed sequence are merged into a single interval. Second, merging normally requires the same canonical motif — the lexicographically smallest rotation among all cyclic permutations of the forward and reverse complement — but for motifs of 50 bp and longer, motifs within 10% Hamming distance are treated as one family. Third, overlap resolution retains the higher-scoring call only when the overlap exceeds half the shorter interval; smaller overlaps leave both calls in the output.

Two supplementary passes address gaps in the core pipeline. A satellite gap-filling mechanism examines uncovered blocks flanking detected satellite arrays (motif of at least 100 bp) and evaluates autocorrelation identity at candidate periods between 100 and 360 bp (Supplementary Methods S1.4). Only uncovered blocks of 300 bp to 100 kb lying within 50 kb of such an anchor are considered, and identity is sampled from up to three 5 kb windows inside the block rather than from the whole of it. If peak autocorrelation exceeds approximately 45% — against the ≈0.26 expected for independent bases at these genomes' composition, and a measured median of 0.268 in maize intergenic sequence (Section 3.3.3) — the entire block is emitted as one call, taking its motif from the sampled window and a mismatch rate of one minus the measured identity, so a call accepted at the floor is reported at 55% divergence. This recovers interior gaps within highly diverged centromeric arrays that retain periodic signal but are too diverged for exact k-mer seeding.

An optional catch-all periodicity sweep evaluates windowed autocorrelation at periods 1–20 bp across uncovered sequence in O(n) time, using a window of twice the candidate period subject to a 12 bp floor. Stretches of at least 20 bp exceeding a minimum identity threshold (default 72%) are reported as additional detections, with the reported interval running to the end of the last passing window, so a call extends up to one window past the last position tested; candidates already more than 30% covered by an existing call are dropped. This mode emphasizes STR recall and is disabled by default.
2.2 Benchmarking Methodology
BWTandem was benchmarked against seven tools: TRF 4.10.0rc2 (Benson, 1999), mreps 2.6.01 (Kolpakov et al., 2003), ULTRA 1.2.1 (Olson and Wheeler, 2024), tantan 51 (Frith, 2011), NCRF 1.01.02 (Harris et al., 2019), TRASH v1 (Wlodzimierz et al., 2023), and TideHunter 1.5.6 (Gao et al., 2019). Every competitor run was executed inside one Singularity image; versions and full command lines are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Six of the seven produced results we report. TideHunter produced no completed run on any of the three genomes and is absent from every table; the runs were abandoned for runtime, but we did not record the wall clock at which, so we can state the exclusion without quantifying it. mreps was excluded from the maize experiments for runtime and did not complete on human (Table 1a). NCRF was excluded on human for memory: it required 80.96 GB on the 132 Mb Arabidopsis assembly (Table 2), and no completed human run exists. TRASH was evaluated in both de novo and template modes on Arabidopsis and maize; on human only the de novo mode was run, so the single human TRASH row is a de novo result. Motif-guided tools were provided exact consensus sequences.

Period search ranges were not uniform across tools, and because this affects which metrics are comparable we state them explicitly. BWTandem was run at 1–2,000 bp on every genome. TRF has no minimum-period parameter and was run to a maximum of 500 bp on human, 200 bp on Arabidopsis, and the maximum matching each maize experiment. ULTRA was run at its default maximum period of 100 bp on human and at 6, 500, and 200 bp for maize experiments 3A, 3B, and 3C. tantan was run at its default maximum period of 100 bp on every genome; that ceiling is tunable, and we did not test a wider one. mreps was run without period bounds on human. On Arabidopsis, ULTRA and mreps at their original settings could not represent a 178 bp monomer — ULTRA at a 100 bp maximum, mreps at 6 bp — so both were re-run, at a 500 bp maximum and over 150–400 bp respectively, and Table 2 reports the re-runs. Search ranges therefore differ by up to twentyfold on human and by more than three hundredfold across the maize experiments. Region-level metrics are affected only marginally, but base pair metrics computed over the full range are not directly comparable between tools, since 35.4% of BWTandem's called bases and 67.6% of TRF's lie above period 100 while ULTRA and tantan report nothing there. Section 3.1 therefore reports a matched-range comparison restricted to periods ≤100 bp, the widest range every tool could enter, alongside the full-range figures and a separate stratum for periods 101–2,000 bp.
2.2.1 Computational Environment and Execution
All benchmarking was performed on the University of Nevada, Reno Pronghorn HPC cluster using Intel Xeon E5-2683 v4 processors at 2.10 GHz, with each genome processed as an independent SLURM job. Allocations differed by tool: the BWTandem runs behind Tables 1a, 1c, 2 and 3 used two cores, the matched-range runs behind Tables 1b and 1d used four, TRF, mreps and tantan one, and the remaining tools two. Cost figures fall into two provenance classes and the distinction should be read into every runtime and memory cell below. Every BWTandem figure was re-measured from a single recorded execution and is traceable in the provenance manifest to a job identifier, an elapsed time and a cgroup peak. The competitor figures are inherited from an earlier benchmarking round whose accounting records no longer exist; the output files and command lines survive, the job entries do not, so those cells carry the wall clock and maximum resident set size that GNU time reported at the time and cannot be re-derived. We report them because they are the best record available and because the accuracy figures they accompany do reproduce, but competitor cost should be read as approximate and BWTandem cost as documented.

BWTandem was configured for the full period range of 1 to 2,000 bp using two threads for the whole-genome comparisons of Tables 1a, 2 and 3, and four threads for the matched-range operating-point runs behind Table 1b, Table 1d and Figure 1. Call count is identical across thread counts, verified at one and two threads (4,009,261 calls in both); we did not check byte-identity of the outputs, and did not test four threads. Thread count changes both cost axes. At the full range on human, a one-thread run took 15 h 12 m at 13.32 GB and a two-thread run 9 h 21 m at 21.38 GB, against 12.1 h at 21.86 GB for the separately executed two-thread run reported in Table 1a; the two two-thread runs differ by 29% in wall clock at an identical call count, which sets the scale of run-to-run variation on this cluster and is a further reason to read the cost tables as approximate. Memory fits a fixed base of about 5 GB plus roughly 8 GB per thread, as expected when one FM-index is held per in-flight chromosome. Tier 1 used FM-index enumeration mode. The supplementary periodicity sweep was enabled for the human and Zea mays genomes with a minimum autocorrelation of 72% and minimum 3 copies, but disabled for Arabidopsis to preserve specificity. Tier 3 used the balanced preset with adaptive parameters. To ensure equitable comparison in the cost tables, multithreaded tools including BWTandem and ULTRA were constrained to two threads. The single-threaded tools were run as single processes; mreps was applied to one sequence at a time in sequence. Runtime for the BWTandem rows is the SLURM accounting elapsed time, corroborated by GNU time where both were recorded; competitor runtime is GNU time wall clock. Peak memory for the BWTandem rows is the SLURM cgroup maximum (sacct MaxRSS), because BWTandem distributes chromosomes across worker processes and GNU time reports the maximum over children rather than their sum, which undercounts a multi-worker run. The competing tools are single processes, so their rows carry the GNU time maximum resident set size, which for them is the same quantity. The human runs predate a fix that stops the satellite gap-filling pass from emitting motifs containing characters other than A, C, G and T. The human output holds 235 such calls spanning 4.81 Mb; 198 of them, spanning 4.57 Mb, come from the gap-filling pass and are removed by the released code, while the remaining 37, spanning 0.24 Mb, originate in Tiers 1 to 3, which that fix does not guard. Removing the 198 raises base pair precision by roughly 0.4 percentage points, so the figures reported here are the conservative ones. The Arabidopsis and maize runs contain no such calls and are unaffected.
2.2.2 Genome Assemblies
Performance was assessed across three genome assemblies representing distinct scales and repeat landscapes. The human GRCh38 reference genome (3.1 Gb) served as the primary benchmark target. The two sides of that comparison used different files, and we state the difference because it bears on the cost figures. The competitors were run on the GenBank FASTA for accession GCA_000001405.15, which carries unplaced scaffolds and alt loci in addition to the chromosomes; BWTandem was run on the 24 primary chromosome sequences of the UCSC hg38 release of the same assembly (chr1–22, X, Y; 3,150,035,398 bytes). Scoring restricts both sides to those 24 chromosomes, so no accuracy figure is affected — tantan, for example, makes 125,983 of its calls outside them, and all are discarded before scoring — but the competitors' runtime and memory cover roughly 5% more sequence than BWTandem's. The telomere to telomere Arabidopsis thaliana Col-CEN v1.2 assembly (132 Mb, Naish et al., 2021) provided a compact genome with well characterized centromeric satellite arrays; the assembly is distributed at https://github.com/schatzlab/Col-CEN, and the reads it was built from are in ArrayExpress under E-MTAB-10272 (Oxford Nanopore) and the European Nucleotide Archive under PRJEB46164 (PacBio HiFi). The Zea mays Mo17 T2T assembly (2.18 Gb, Chen et al., 2023, GenBank accession GCA_022117705.1) served as a large, repeat rich crop genome with multiple annotated satellite families.
2.2.3 Evaluation Metrics
For the human genome (Experiment 1), accuracy was evaluated against release v1.2.1 of the GIAB adotto tandem repeat region catalog (English et al., 2024; https://zenodo.org/records/13987414), restricted to chr1–22, X and Y. Region-level recall and precision were defined as the fraction of benchmark or tool-predicted regions overlapping at least one counterpart by at least one base pair (`bedtools intersect -u`); no reciprocal-overlap fraction was required. That criterion is generous to tools reporting few long intervals and should be read alongside the call counts, which differ by three orders of magnitude across the tools compared, and alongside the period-dependent merge described in Section 2.1.5, under which one BWTandem interval can satisfy several benchmark regions. Base pair recall and precision were computed on merged intervals and are not subject to that effect. Because the GIAB catalog is known to be incomplete, we computed an adjusted precision in which predictions not overlapping any benchmark region were tested for corroboration by ULTRA and tantan, and corroborated predictions were reclassified as true positives. A tool cannot corroborate its own calls, so the ULTRA and tantan rows have one corroborator each while the remaining rows have two; adjusted precision is consequently not directly comparable across rows of Tables 1a and 1e, and we also report a symmetric leave-one-out variant in which every other tool acts as a corroborator. Neither corroborator searched above period 100, so neither can corroborate a call above it; we verified that this biases the figure by only +0.11 percentage points, since such calls are 2.5% of the total. The number of unique regions detected exclusively by each tool was also recorded.

For Arabidopsis thaliana (Experiment 2), performance was measured as mean centromere coverage across five centromeric regions (Naish et al., 2021), computed with no period filter. CEN180 monomer recall was computed against reference monomer coordinates obtained by aligning a 177 bp CEN178 consensus to the Col-CEN v1.2 assembly with blastn. The blastn version and command line were not retained, so we deposit the inputs and outputs rather than the invocation: the consensus sequence, the 68,840 raw hits, and the 66,683 monomers that survive filtering, which span 175–179 bp at 80–100% identity. Every row of Table 2 is scored against that one coordinate set, so no comparison in this paper depends on the lost command; a reader regenerating the coordinates from scratch would, however, have to choose their own parameters and should expect a slightly different set. The two CEN180 columns of Table 2 use different call sets, applied identically to every row: monomer recall accepts a call of any reported period, while the CEN180 count admits only calls of reported period 150–200 bp. The banded rule is period-assignment sensitive — a tool that calls the array as its ~356 bp dimer covers it perfectly and still scores zero — so Section 3.2 reports monomer recall under the unfiltered rule, the 150–200 bp band, and a 150–400 bp band that admits the dimer.

For Zea mays (Experiment 3), three aspects were evaluated. Experiment 3A measured microsatellite detection (periods ≤6 bp), with TAG arrays as diagnostic markers of heterochromatic knob repeats. Two scoring rules are in play here and both are reported. The rule used in our earlier analysis applied the 1–6 bp period band to the BWTandem row alone, scoring every other tool on its whole output, and counted a TAG array as any call whose motif column contains the substring TAG or CTA; Table 3A retains it so the two are comparable. Table 3A-b applies the band to every tool and counts a TAG array as a call of period 3 whose leading trinucleotide is a rotation of TAG. Table 3A-b is the rule we would ask a reader to use. Experiment 3B evaluated satellite detection against curated coordinates for 25 knob180 (~180 bp) and 17 TR-1 (~358 bp) arrays from Chen et al. (2023). Metrics included detection sensitivity, mean boundary offset error, and a normalized fragmentation score (1.0 = single contiguous call per array). Experiment 3C assessed 17 CentC centromeric arrays (~156 bp) using the same metrics plus centromere coverage. mreps was excluded from maize experiments; NCRF was included only in Experiment 3A.

Every figure in this paper is a single-run point estimate, and we report no confidence intervals or significance tests. Each tool is deterministic given its inputs, so the estimates carry no sampling error in the usual sense; what they lack is any estimate of how much a difference between two tools would move under a different benchmark, a different chromosome set, or a different curation of the ground truth. The differences we build arguments on are large — a twentyfold range difference in cost, a 24-point recall gap between BWTandem and TRF, the presence or absence of any call above period 100. Several differences reported below are not: the four-way spread in Arabidopsis centromere coverage is 0.20 percentage points, the CentC coverage lead is 0.35, and the gap between BWTandem's most sensitive setting and ULTRA on region recall is 0.02. We describe those as ties rather than as rankings, and no claim in the Discussion rests on one. The provenance manifest names the scoring script and its hash for every table, so a reader wishing to resample the ground truth can rerun the same code.
3. Results
3.1 Tandem Repeat Detection on Human GRCh38
Six tools were compared on the 3.1 Gb human GRCh38 genome using the GIAB adotto benchmark (Table 1a). TideHunter was excluded due to excessive runtime, and NCRF was excluded due to excessive memory consumption.
 
 The cost of searching a wide period range was measured directly, by running BWTandem twice on the same genome, same code and same thread count, changing only the ceiling. Both runs used four threads. Searching 1–2,000 bp took 7 h 23 m against 5 h 17 m for 1–100 bp: a twentyfold wider search for 1.40 times the runtime and no additional memory (35.28 against 35.24 GB). These are not the numbers in Table 1a, which reports the two-thread run of the same configuration at 12.1 h; the range-cost comparison needs a fixed thread count and the cross-tool comparison needs the two-thread allocation, so they are different executions of identical settings and both are recorded in the manifest. A tool that scans per period pays more than linearly by comparison — ULTRA on maize goes from 0.23 h at a maximum period of 6 bp to 34.7 h at 500 bp, an 83-fold range increase for a 151-fold cost increase. Seeding candidate positions once from the index and extending only confirmed candidates is what decouples the two, and it is why BWTandem can be run at its full range by default rather than at a range the user has to guess. The index depends only on the sequence and not on any detection setting, so a sensitivity sweep can reuse it rather than rebuilding it per point; an on-disk cache loads a stored index in 0.6 s where construction takes 9 s on the Arabidopsis assembly and 21 minutes across the 24 human chromosomes. Construction is 0.4% of a Col-CEN run and 2.9 to 7.4% of a human one, so reuse is a convenience for sweeps rather than a material part of the cost, and the cost figures reported here are for runs that each built their own index. Against the competitors on this genome, it searched 1–2,000 bp in 12.1 hours while ULTRA took 29.8 hours restricted to 1–100 bp and TRF 33.7 hours at a 500 bp maximum.

	 Within the range all tools shared, periods of 100 bp or less, the three high-recall methods are close and ULTRA is marginally ahead: region recall 81.62% for ULTRA, 79.88% for BWTandem, 78.00% for tantan, with TRF at 30.80% (Table 1b). ULTRA also leads base pair recall at 38.14% against BWTandem's 32.72%. Above period 100 the field thins to three: ULTRA and tantan, run at their default 100 bp ceilings, report nothing there, and of the three that do, BWTandem recovers the most — 3.44% of benchmark regions and 13.44% of benchmark bases in that stratum at 65.97% region precision, in the same single pass (Table 1c). TRF reaches 2.02% and 11.83% there, more precisely but after a 33.7 hour run capped at 500 bp, and TRASH 0.25% and 5.23%. The full-range base pair figures — 43.42% for BWTandem against ULTRA's 38.14% — combine both strata and are reported for completeness only; they measure search range as much as sensitivity. mreps did not complete on this assembly and is excluded (Table 1a).

	 BWTandem does not occupy one point in this space. Its sensitivity is set by an optional periodicity pass with a tunable identity threshold, so a user selects an operating point at run time, and we report the whole curve (Figure 1, Table 1d). Region recall spans 54.69% to 81.60% and region precision 62.17% down to 48.43%. At the conservative end BWTandem-P reaches 62.17% region precision, the highest of any method recovering more than half the benchmark: tantan manages 57.66% and ULTRA 53.66%, and the only tools above it, TRF and TRASH, recover 30.80% and 0.91% of the catalog respectively. At the aggressive end BWTandem-H matches ULTRA's region recall to 0.02 percentage points, at 5.2 points lower precision, and the intermediate settings sit between. These four configurations differ in more than the threshold — P both disables the pass and omits the relaxed short-period gate, B caps the swept period at 50 bp, and F additionally requires three copies (Supplementary Methods S2) — so Table 1d is not a single-parameter sweep. A true sweep, holding every other setting fixed and moving only the identity threshold, moves region recall from 62.59% with the pass disabled to 81.46% at identity 0.68, and region precision from 54.77% down to 48.94%. That sweep runs over the full 1–2,000 bp range and is scored by a different script from Table 1d, so its absolute values are not directly comparable with the matched-range panel; the shape is what it establishes. The response is not linear in the threshold: identities 0.80 and 0.76 land within 0.2 points of each other on both axes, and the largest single step, 9.0 of the 18.9 points of recall the sweep spans, arrives between 0.76 and 0.72. In base pair space ULTRA leads on recall at 38.14% against BWTandem's best of 33.57%, while BWTandem-P is more precise than ULTRA at 62.56% against 61.33%, though tantan at 70.22% and TRF at 93.16% are more precise still on far smaller outputs; the aggressive settings sit well below both. We did not sweep the competitors' own sensitivity parameters, so the asymmetry between a curve and a set of points reflects how the comparison was run rather than a property the other tools lack.

	 The periodicity pass is what converts precision into recall, and its cost falls almost entirely on base pair precision: 62.56% with the pass disabled against 34–35% with it enabled at any threshold, for a 6.8 point gain in base pair recall. Classifying every called base by its distance to the nearest benchmark region shows where those bases go. Bases lying outside a benchmark region but within 500 bp of one — the ones a boundary trim would recover — are 13.8% of BWTandem's output with the pass enabled, the lowest of the three tools for which we ran this decomposition and below ULTRA's 19.7% and tantan's 14.8%; TRF and TRASH were not decomposed. Bases more than 500 bp from any region rise from 20.1% with the pass disabled, in line with ULTRA at 19.0% and tantan at 15.0%, to 51.7% with it enabled. The pass therefore does not extend existing calls; it makes new ones, in sequence the catalog does not cover. Of the 123.7 Mb it adds, 96.3 Mb falls in that far class, and ULTRA and tantan together report only 40.1 Mb of far sequence in total, so most of it is called by BWTandem and by nothing else in this comparison. The pass is disabled by default for this reason.

	 Adjusted precision, which credits predictions corroborated by an independent tool, is insensitive to this choice: it peaks mid-curve at 79.83% and varies by 2.7 points across settings whose raw precision spans 13.7 points, because the calls added at lower thresholds are disproportionately ones ULTRA or tantan also report. Under the same rule tantan reaches 82.93% and ULTRA 78.44% (Table 1e). On the full-range run, 58.5% of BWTandem's predictions outside the adotto catalog are corroborated by ULTRA or tantan, which places much of the apparent precision gap in catalog incompleteness rather than in false discovery. Section 4.2 shows that under the symmetric leave-one-out rule the same measurement is higher for ULTRA and tantan than for BWTandem, so this does not distinguish the tools in BWTandem's favour.

  BWTandem identified more unique regions — regions not called by any other tool — than any other tool, roughly 1.7 times the count unique to tantan or ULTRA.
   
   BWTandem's runtime of 12.1 hours came with a memory cost. Its FM index required more memory than any other tool run on this genome, an order of magnitude more than TRF or tantan; larger footprints appear elsewhere in this paper, from NCRF on Arabidopsis and TRASH on maize. tantan finished fastest, at 23.54% base pair recall against BWTandem's 43.42%, though the two are within 2.5 points at region level. mreps was comparably fast but did not complete, so no recall figure exists for it.

   3.2 Centromeric Satellite Detection in Arabidopsis thaliana
   The Arabidopsis thaliana Col CEN v1.2 assembly (132 Mb) provided a particularly informative test case because its five centromeres are dominated by well characterized CEN180 satellite arrays (Table 2).
    
	BWTandem processed this 132 Mb assembly in 31 minutes searching 1–2,000 bp. TRF, capped at 200 bp, required 131.9 hours — more than 250 times longer on a genome an order of magnitude smaller than human, where the same tool took 33.7 hours. ULTRA needed 10.1 hours once raised to a 500 bp maximum, 20 times BWTandem's single pass. Runtime on satellite-dense sequence is where the index-based approach separates from alignment-based and model-based ones, and it does so by a margin no accuracy figure in this section approaches.

	 On accuracy the tools converge to the point where the differences are not interpretable. Centromere coverage spans 0.20 percentage points across BWTandem (84.59%), ULTRA (84.44%), and TRF (84.39%), with TRASH at 85.03% and NCRF at 85.17%; we read the first three as a tie. At the monomer level BWTandem recovers 99.73% of BLAST-derived CEN180 monomers and ULTRA 99.80%, matching NCRF at 99.63%, which was given the consensus, and TRASH at 99.69%, which reaches the same figure in de novo mode without one. BWTandem's CEN180 base pair precision, 57.17%, is the lowest of the tools that detect the arrays at all; the re-run mreps reaches 98.33%. On this genome the claim is speed, not detection.

	 The comparison required re-running two tools, and the asymmetry is worth stating. At their original settings ULTRA and mreps returned 2.25% and 1.58% centromere coverage — figures forced by a maximum period of 100 bp and 6 bp respectively, neither of which can represent a 178 bp monomer. Raising ULTRA's maximum to 500 bp and running mreps over 150–400 bp lifted them to 84.44% and 58.48%. Only mreps needed a bracketing window; ULTRA needed only a higher ceiling, and was not told where to look. TRF was left at its original 200 bp maximum, which can hold the monomer but not the ~356 bp dimer. We did not re-run the tools that scored well, so the re-runs are asymmetric in BWTandem's disfavour on accuracy and in its favour on nothing.

	 Reported period is a property of the caller as much as of the array, and it moves these numbers more than detection does. Restricting calls to periods of 150–200 bp drops ULTRA to 73.36% monomer recall and BWTandem to 91.92%, while TRF is unchanged at 97.55%; widening to 150–400 bp returns ULTRA to 99.79%. ULTRA calls CEN180 predominantly as its approximately 356 bp dimer, BWTandem does so for a minority of arrays, and TRF resolves the monomer. Any analysis that selects calls by an expected monomer length therefore measures period assignment alongside detection.

	 The satellite gap-fill pass contributes materially to these figures, and its contribution is not an artefact of its default period window. The pass closes uncovered gaps between satellite calls when the gap sequence is periodic, using a window of 100–360 bp chosen for the human alpha-satellite monomer and its dimer — a window that also brackets CEN180 at 178 and 356 bp. The ablation was run against a defaults configuration rather than the Table 2 row, and the three arms are therefore internally comparable but offset from the published numbers: the base scores 84.77% coverage and 99.76% monomer recall, against 84.59% and 99.73% in Table 2. Relative to that base, disabling the pass costs 5.82 percentage points of centromere coverage and 6.37 points of monomer recall, so the headline figure is a pipeline-plus-gap-fill result rather than a three-tier one. Moving the window to 400–1,000 bp, which cannot contain either 178 or 356, costs 0.54 and 0.47 points. The mechanism is therefore periodicity at multiples of the monomer rather than a constant tuned to this satellite.

	  3.3 Repeat Detection across Multiple Scales in Zea mays Mo17
	  The Zea mays Mo17 T2T assembly was used to evaluate detection across three repeat classes (Tables 3A through 3C). mreps was excluded due to time overflow, and NCRF was included only for TAG microsatellite evaluation.
	  3.3.1 Microsatellite Detection
	  With a 1–6 bp band applied to every tool alike, microsatellite yield spans an order of magnitude: ULTRA 46.96 Mb, BWTandem 41.31 Mb, tantan 13.70 Mb, TRF 4.79 Mb (Table 3A-b). BWTandem reports the most regions of any tool, 1.51 million against ULTRA's 1.11 million, so it resolves the same class of sequence into more and shorter arrays. NCRF and TRASH emit a consensus rather than a primitive period and are not comparable under a microsatellite band.

	   For TAG trinucleotide arrays, a diagnostic marker of maize heterochromatic knob repeats, the count depends on how a TAG array is defined. Counting calls of period 3 whose leading trinucleotide is a rotation of TAG gives BWTandem 34,247, tantan 11,752, ULTRA 7,031, and TRF 1,379. Counting instead any call whose motif column contains the substring TAG or CTA — the definition used in the earlier analysis — changes each tool's count by a different factor, because a long consensus contains those trinucleotides by chance while a period-3 call may begin at a rotation the substring test misses. Scored without a period band, the substring rule gives tantan 181,205 against 11,752 under the rotation rule, a factor of 15.4, and BWTandem 474,410 against 34,247, a factor of 13.9; TRF moves by 3.8 and ULTRA moves the other way, from 4,554 to 7,031. The two definitions give different orderings — tantan > BWTandem > TRF > ULTRA under the substring rule against BWTandem > tantan > ULTRA > TRF under the rotation rule — with both adjacent pairs swapped. We adopted the rotation rule, and report it alongside the original rather than in place of it: Table 3A retains the substring rule and Table 3A-b restates the comparison under the rotation rule, so a reader can see both. NCRF, given the TAG motif as input, was the fastest tool in this experiment at 0.13 h against ULTRA's 0.2 h, but required 17.47 GB, the second-largest footprint here; a targeted query is cheap in time and is not applicable to de novo discovery.

	   3.3.2 Satellite Array Detection (knob180 and TR-1)
	   BWTandem, TRF, ULTRA, and TRASH each detected all 25 knob180 and all 17 TR-1 arrays (Table 3B); tantan missed one knob180 array. On TR-1, the harder of the two classes, BWTandem gives the highest coverage of any tool that also places boundaries accurately: 50.49% against TRF's 45.77% and ULTRA's 36.36%. TRASH covers marginally more, 53.17%, with a mean boundary offset of 10.6 kb against BWTandem's 0.9 kb, so its extra coverage is flanking sequence rather than array. On knob180 the four tools sit within 2.6 points of each other, 78.71% to 81.23%, with BWTandem at 80.32%; that spread is small enough that we read the class as a four-way tie on coverage. Boundary offsets rank ULTRA (203 bp), BWTandem (275 bp), TRF (652 bp), then TRASH (9.8 kb). On fragmentation the ordering reverses: TRASH is the least fragmented at roughly twenty calls per array, giving a normalized score of 0.05, while every other tool including BWTandem splits each array into hundreds — BWTandem 253 to 475 calls per array depending on the class, ULTRA 303 to 878, and TRF as many as 1,705 on knob180 — giving a normalized score that rounds to zero. The calls-per-array counts quoted here are the reciprocals of the Frag. Score column, taken from the same scoring run; the column itself rounds to two decimals and so cannot distinguish them. The score is the reciprocal of calls per array, so one call per array would be 1.00 and no tool here approaches it. Coverage and boundary accuracy therefore come at the cost of contiguity, and an analysis needing one interval per array will have to merge.

Fourteen of the 59 arrays have calls reaching more than 500 bp beyond the curated interval, eleven of them TR-1; measuring windowed autocorrelation identity at the family period across those flanks gives a median of 0.430, against 0.268 for intergenic sequence sampled on the same chromosomes and interior medians of 0.367, 0.329, and 0.405 for knob180, TR-1, and CentC. The curated coordinates are conservative at these loci, and a boundary trimming step would remove sequence carrying the family period. The remaining 45 arrays have no flank of this size to measure, which is the same fact the sub-kilobase mean offsets report.

		 4. Discussion
		 4.1 Practical significance for de novo genome annotation
		 NCRF requires a curated motif library to exist before it can be run at all; the de novo detectors do not, but they do require the user to bound the period range in advance, which for an uncharacterised satellite is a related problem. BWTandem instead moves repeat discovery earlier in the annotation process. Candidate arrays can be located directly from an assembly before any species specific library has been built — grouping them into families is a further step this work does not evaluate. The three genomes benchmarked here are among the minority that already have curated satellite coordinates, which is why they can serve as ground truth at all: adotto for human (English et al., 2024), CEN180 for Arabidopsis (Naish et al., 2021), and the knob180, TR-1 and CentC arrays for maize (Chen et al., 2023). That is the situation a tool of this kind is not built for. The case for it rests on the assemblies being completed without such catalogs, and how large that set is relative to the annotated one is something we assert rather than measure.
		 4.2 Why recall over precision
		 A case can be made for favouring recall in first-pass annotation of an uncharacterised genome, which is a different task from annotating a final structural variant call set. A missed array may never enter downstream analyses unless detection is repeated under different assumptions, while a spurious call is one candidate among many that filtering, orthogonal evidence, or curation can remove. The adotto benchmark is itself incomplete, so a tool biased toward completeness has some chance of surfacing catalog gaps rather than only reproducing an existing standard.

		 That argument does not license the operating points at the aggressive end of our curve, and we do not use it that way. A recall-precision trade-off is only a trade if something is gained: BWTandem-H reaches ULTRA's region recall to within 0.02 percentage points but at 5.2 points lower precision, and BWTandem-F trails ULTRA on both axes, so at those settings nothing is being bought. The same holds on Arabidopsis, where ULTRA leads on monomer recall and on base pair precision. The trade is real only at the conservative end, where disabling the periodicity pass gives the highest region precision of any method here that recovers more than half the benchmark, and on the period range above 100 bp, which only TRF and TRASH also entered and where BWTandem recovers 3.44% of the catalog against their 2.02% and 0.25%. Those are the results the recall argument supports; the rest of the curve is offered as a choice a user can make, not as a claim that the choice is favourable.

		 The corroboration analysis does not settle this either, and it does not run in BWTandem's favour. Under the published rule, 58.5% of BWTandem's out-of-catalog predictions are also called by ULTRA or tantan, against 53.5% and 59.7% for those two — but that rule gives BWTandem two corroborators and each of them only one, so the comparison is not like-for-like. Under the leave-one-out rule, where every tool has the same four corroborators, BWTandem's figure is essentially unchanged at 59.2% while ULTRA's and tantan's rise to roughly 71.5% and 71.6%. BWTandem's out-of-catalog calls are therefore the least corroborated of the three, not the most. Catalog incompleteness raises all of these figures rather than closing the gap between them, and in any case the corroborating tools are de novo detectors responding to the same periodicity signal, not independent evidence.
		 4.3 Comparison with existing approaches
		 Tandem repeats have a structural property that alignment based methods do not exploit directly. A sampled kmer drawn from within a repeat array recurs at every copy, so the occurrence positions falling inside that array form an approximate arithmetic progression whose common difference is the period of the array itself. Periodicity can therefore be read directly from a sorted list of occurrence positions before any alignment takes place. Alignment based methods reach the same signal less directly. TRF, for instance, also derives candidate periods from the spacing of matching k-tuples before aligning, so the difference is not that the information is unavailable to them. It is that an FM index locates the query in the index in time proportional to the kmer length alone, independent of the size of the genome and of how often the kmer occurs, and then yields the occurrence list at a cost proportional to its length — where a scan pays for the sequence whether or not the kmer is there. That is what allows BWTandem to identify candidate periods before committing to the alignment step that dominates the cost of TRF and related tools.
		 The sharpest cost contrast in these experiments is between the two genomes, and it is about TRF rather than about BWTandem. TRF took 131.9 hours on the 132 Mb Arabidopsis genome and 5.5 hours on the 2.18 Gb maize genome, a genome sixteen times larger, both runs capped at the same maximum period of 200 bp. This is consistent with TRF's cost tracking repeat density rather than genome size, with dense CEN180 arrays the most plausible driver given how concentrated they are in the Arabidopsis assembly, a pattern already noted for TRF on satellite rich sequence generally (Wlodzimierz et al., 2023). BWTandem does not show that inversion — 0.51 hours on Arabidopsis against 15.4 hours on maize, a thirtyfold increase for a sixteenfold size increase, so it is superlinear in genome size on this pair and we have no evidence that it is density-insensitive in general. What the pair does establish is that the tool most exposed to satellite density is the alignment-based one, which is the direction an indexing based approach predicts; we have not isolated the effect to kmer seeding specifically, and a two-genome contrast in one tool cannot.
		 4.4 Limitations
		 Three limitations bound the results above. The first is period assignment. Filtering calls by reported period costs BWTandem 15.6, 33.0, and 17.8 percentage points of coverage on knob180, TR-1, and CentC, against at most 1.5 for TRF and 2.8 for ULTRA (Tables 3B-b, 3C-b): it covers these arrays as well as any tool tested but frequently assigns a period outside the nominal monomer band, so an analysis selecting calls by expected monomer length will discard correct detections. Whether those periods are harmonics of the true monomer, and whether post-processing could recover the nominal period without re-running detection, remains open; the analogous question at the period-100 boundary in human was tested directly and the harmonic explanation did not hold, so it should not be assumed here.

		 The second is memory. BWTandem holds one FM-index per in-flight chromosome, so its footprint is the largest of the de novo tools compared — 21.86 GB on human at two threads against ULTRA's 1.68 GB and tantan's 0.27 GB — and grows with thread count rather than being fixed: measured on human at the full range, 13.32 GB at one thread and 21.38 GB at two, a fixed base near 5 GB plus roughly 8 GB per thread (Section 2.2.1). It is not the largest figure in these experiments: the template-guided NCRF required 80.96 GB on Arabidopsis, and TRASH in template mode required 120.6 GB on maize. The distinction matters for a user choosing between them, since BWTandem's requirement is tunable downward by running fewer threads while NCRF's is not.

		 The third concerns the regions BWTandem reports that no other tool does — 875,224 calls, 21.9% of its output, measured on the matched-range run of Tables 1b and 1d-F against ULTRA, tantan, TRF and TRASH, and characterised in Supplementary Table S2. These are not the low-complexity artefacts such a count usually indicates: their median length is within 1 bp of the calls the other tools share, and their mean motif entropy is higher, 1.24 bits against 1.03, with fewer of them below 1 bit. They skew toward longer periods than the shared calls, not shorter: the 21-100 bp band holds 21.9% of them against 6.1% of shared calls, while 13.2% are mononucleotide or dinucleotide against 24.8%. 91% of them lie outside the adotto catalog, a fraction unchanged by whether the periodicity pass is enabled. This benchmark cannot adjudicate them. They are absent from the catalog and from every other tool's output, and some of that absence is by design, since tantan masks rather than reports and TRF's thresholds exclude short arrays. Establishing whether they are detections or over-calls requires evidence this comparison does not contain, such as orthogonal validation on the same loci or a catalog built without the coverage assumptions adotto makes.
		 4.5 Future directions
		 A compressed occurrence structure, such as a wavelet tree rank index (Grossi et al., 2003), could reduce memory use without changing the underlying seeding algorithm, making the tool practical on genomes substantially larger than the three tested here. Beyond implementation, the periodic flanking sequence measured here points at a biological question. On the fourteen of fifty-nine curated maize arrays where BWTandem extends more than 500 bp past the curated coordinates, the extension carries the family period: thirteen of the fourteen exceed the 95th percentile of intergenic background, at a median identity of 0.430 that is above every family's interior median. At those loci the curated boundary looks like a scoring decision rather than a sequence transition. The base is narrow, though, and the observation is largely about one family — eleven of the fourteen flanks are TR-1, and the single CentC flank is the exception at 0.269, indistinguishable from the 0.268 intergenic median and the only one of the fourteen not to clear the 95th percentile of background. Establishing how general that is would require the same measurement across curated satellite catalogs in other species, and would bear on how array boundaries are defined rather than on any one tool. The unique regions absent from the adotto catalog offer a similarly direct test of whether the human tandem repeat catalog is systematically missing particular repeat classes or genomic contexts, and are now characterised well enough to design it: longer periods than the corroborated calls, higher motif diversity, and 91% outside the catalog. Extending the approach to noisy long reads, as TideHunter does (Gao et al., 2019), would carry the same recall favoring logic into repeat genotyping ahead of assembly, which is a different setting from annotation of an already finished genome.
		 A tool built to be useful before a curated repeat catalog exists is answering a different question than a tool built to refine calls within one. If T2T assemblies continue to accumulate across non model species faster than repeat libraries can be built for them — which we expect but have not quantified — that distinction will matter more than which tool reports the tightest boundary on any single benchmark.

		 Supplementary Methods
		 S1. BWTandem Parameter Specifications
		 S1.1 Tier 1: Short Tandem Repeat Detection
		 A Tier 1 candidate of period p and length L base pairs is retained when both of the following hold.

		 L ≥ max(L_min, p · C_min(p))     and     Q = L · (1 − e) ≥ Q_min

		 Here e is the mismatch rate, Q is the quality score used throughout the pipeline, and C_min is a period-dependent minimum copy count.

		 C_min(p) = max(2, ⌊6/p⌋ + 2)

		 This gives 8 copies for mononucleotide repeats, 5 for dinucleotides, 4 for trinucleotides, 3 for periods of 4 to 6 bp, and 2 for 7 to 9 bp. The implementation's built-in defaults are max(3, ⌊12/p⌋ + 3); the values above are the settings used for every run reported in this paper, applied through the TIER1_COPYBASE, TIER1_COPYADD and TIER1_MIN_COPIES environment overrides.

		 The length floor L_min and score floor Q_min are 17 for the runs using the relaxed short-period gate and 20 for the BWTandem-P configuration, which predates it. The copy-count term is therefore not the binding constraint for most periods: at L_min = 17, a mononucleotide array must reach 17 copies rather than 8, a dinucleotide 9 rather than 5, and a trinucleotide 6 rather than 4. The copy count binds only at p = 9, where p · C_min(p) = 18. A reader tuning sensitivity should treat the length and score floors as the primary levers, not the copy-count formula.

		 In the configuration used throughout this paper the FM-index enumeration runs as a second, additive pass: the sliding-window scanner runs first under the gate above, and the enumeration then scans only the residual sequence that pass did not already claim, so it contributes the diverged short arrays an exact-seed scan cannot reach without double-reporting the ones it can. It enumerates every primitive motif rotation of length 1 to 6 bp, rotations not being collapsed to a canonical form, and applies its own acceptance gate rather than the one above: at least three occurrences of the motif, a span of at least 20 bp, a log-likelihood ratio for periodic hit testing of 6.0 against an independent-bases null whose base frequencies are estimated from a subsample of the sequence, and a minimum density of 0.45.
		 S1.2 Tier 2: Medium-Length Repeat Detection
		 In the LCP-driven phase, the detected motif is reduced to its primitive period using exact divisibility checks and approximate periodicity tests with a tolerance of 2%, implemented via autocorrelation.

		 In the BWT k-mer seeding phase, k-mers of 6–10 base pairs are sampled at intervals of 5–10 base pairs and progressions are accepted within a 3% jitter tolerance. The mismatch tolerance for Tier 2 was set to 30% in every run reported here, against a library default of 20%, and the same rate applies to both phases. The seeding phase covers periods of 10–50 bp and the strict LCP phase periods of 20 bp and above.
		 S1.3 Tier 3: Adaptive Parameter Computation and Clamping
		 The GC-content-dependent mismatch rate is computed as follows.

		 m = 0.15 + 0.10 · |GC − 0.5|

		 The GC term is bounded in practice by genomic composition: |GC − 0.5| reaches 0.5 only at 0% or 100% GC, so the formula's nominal 20% ceiling is unreachable on real sequence and the realised values are 0.159 for human, 0.164 for Arabidopsis and 0.153 for maize.

		 For sequences exceeding 100 Mb, a large-chromosome mode caps the maximum k-mer occurrence count at 500 and imposes a minimum stride of 150 and a minimum k-mer size of 20. These caps are applied before clamping. The occurrence cap binds on every chromosome in all three genomes; the stride and k-mer minima do not, because the base formulas already exceed them at this scale.

		 All adaptive parameters are then clamped to empirically validated ranges. The rightmost column gives the value in force on the chromosomes of the three genomes benchmarked here, where every parameter is saturated against a bound rather than set by its formula.

| Parameter | Minimum | Maximum | Value used here |
|---|--:|--:|--:|
| K-mer size | 12 | 28 | 28 |
| Sampling stride | 20 | 300 | 300 |
| Max k-mer occurrences | 200 | 1,500 | 500 (large-chromosome cap) |
| Mismatch rate | 15% | 20% | 15.3–16.4% |

		 One consequence is that the coverage-dependent stride reduction described in Section 2.1.4 is inert at chromosome scale: halving a base stride of n / 40,000 still exceeds the 300 ceiling for any sequence above roughly 24 Mb, so the clamp binds first and the stride is 300 regardless of prior tier coverage.

		 The anchor-based boundary verification threshold for large arrays ranges from 70% to 80% match fraction, as determined by the adaptive parameter computation. For smaller arrays, banded Needleman-Wunsch alignment is applied copy by copy in the 5′-to-3′ direction against a consensus that is updated after each accepted copy, so the procedure is a rolling consensus rather than an independent alignment per copy and its result depends on copy order. The band width is min(10, ⌊p/2⌋), and total cost is proportional to the number of copies multiplied by the period length and band width.
		 S1.4 Post-Processing: Autocorrelation Identity
		 The autocorrelation identity at period p over a window W of length w is defined as follows.

		 A(W, p) = (1 / (w − p)) · Σᵢ₌₀^{w−p−1} 𝟙[W[i] = W[i + p]]

		 where 𝟙 denotes the indicator function. For satellite gap-filling, the default minimum identity threshold is 45%; only uncovered blocks of 300 bp to 100 kb within 50 kb of an anchoring call are examined, identity is sampled from at most three 5 kb windows within the block with an early exit once one reaches 0.80, and the accepted block is emitted whole. For the catch-all periodicity sweep, windows of twice the candidate period length, subject to a 12 bp floor, are evaluated using a cumulative sum technique, enabling O(n) computation across all positions; accepted stretches must span at least 20 bp, are reported out to the end of the last passing window, and are discarded if more than 30% of the span is already covered by an existing call.
		 S1.5 Index Storage
		 The suffix array is stored as 32-bit integers and the BWT as an 8-bit character array. Occurrence counts are maintained through checkpointed arrays sampled at fixed intervals, enabling rank queries with constant-time checkpoint lookups followed by short linear scans.
