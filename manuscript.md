Abstract

**Motivation:** Tandem repeat periods run from one base pair in mononucleotide microsatellites to satellite monomers of hundreds of base pairs, and kilobases in some families; none of the detectors we benchmarked covers that span cheaply at the settings tested (we left tantan at its default 100 bp ceiling and did not measure it wider). They take a maximum period, and what a wide one costs is tool-specific and not predictable from the tool's description: on the maize genome ULTRA takes 0.23 hours at a maximum period of 6 bp and 34.7 hours at 500 bp, while TRF over the same span is flat at 5.2 and 5.5 hours. A user searching for an uncharacterised satellite must nonetheless bound its monomer length in advance — the quantity a de novo scan is meant to discover — and, not knowing which case they are in, must bound it conservatively; we capped our own TRF runs at 500 bp on human and 200 bp on Arabidopsis for that reason.

**Results:** We present BWTandem, which seeds candidate repeats from FM-index queries for sparsely sampled k-mers and aligns only confirmed candidates, making detection cost largely independent of the period range requested. Widening its own search twentyfold, from 1–100 bp to 1–2,000 bp on human GRCh38, at a fixed thread count, costs 1.40 times the runtime — a single-pair measurement, read against the 29% same-configuration run-to-run spread Section 2.2.1 reports — and no measurable additional memory, where ULTRA, measured on a different genome and range multiplier and so indicative rather than matched, paid more than linearly: an 83-fold range increase (ratio of maximum periods) for a 151-fold cost increase. Against the competitors it searched 1–2,000 bp in 12.1 hours while ULTRA took 29.8 hours restricted to 1–100 bp and TRF 33.7 hours at a 500 bp maximum (24.2, 59.6 and 33.7 core-hours; BWTandem and ULTRA on two threads, TRF on one). Against the GIAB adotto benchmark, restricted to the period range every tool searched, it recovers 79.9% of catalogued regions to ULTRA's 81.6%, at 50.6% region precision to ULTRA's 53.7% — behind on both axes at that setting (Section 4.2). Above period 100, where ULTRA and tantan, run at their default 100 bp ceilings, report nothing, it reaches 3.4% of the catalog against TRF's 2.0% — TRF capped at 500 bp against BWTandem's 2,000, so that pair is not range-matched. Sensitivity is a run-time choice: four configurations trace an operating point range from 54.7% region recall at 62.2% precision — the highest raw region precision of any method here recovering more than half the benchmark, though under the corroboration-adjusted rule the ordering reverses — 77.1% against ULTRA's 78.4% and tantan's 82.9% (Table 1d) — and the point at which the optional periodicity pass is off — to 81.6% recall at 48.4% — within 0.02 points of ULTRA's, at 5.2 points lower precision; the 79.9% above is the third of the four. On the Arabidopsis Col-CEN centromeres it recovers 99.7% of CEN180 monomers in 31 minutes, matching NCRF at 99.6%, which was given the consensus, TRASH at 99.7%, which was not, and ULTRA at 99.8% in 10.1 hours once its ceiling was raised to admit the 178 bp monomer; TRF, whose 200 bp cap already admitted it, reached 97.6% in 131.9 hours. On maize its CentC array coverage is the highest measured, though by 0.35 percentage points over the runner-up and without an uncertainty estimate — a margin we treat as a tie rather than a ranking — and at the cost of calls fragmented into hundreds of intervals per array; that fragmentation is shared by every de novo tool except TRASH, which is an order of magnitude less fragmented but still reports roughly twenty intervals per array, so an analysis needing one interval per array must merge. Its memory footprint is the largest of the de novo tools compared on the two large genomes (on Arabidopsis, ULTRA's exceeds it) and grows with thread count, at about 8 GB per worker over a fixed base of roughly 5 GB on human GRCh38, the one genome where scaling was measured.

1. Introduction
Tandem repeats—contiguous copies of a DNA sequence motif arranged head-to-tail—are ubiquitous in eukaryotic genomes. They range from microsatellites with 1–6 bp repeat units to megabase-scale satellite arrays that define centromeric and pericentromeric chromatin (Benson, 1999; Naish et al., 2021). Tandem repeats contribute to genome instability through replication slippage and unequal crossing over, are implicated in dozens of heritable human diseases through pathogenic repeat expansions, and serve as substrates for kinetochore assembly in centromeres (Willard, 1998; Mirkin, 2007). Their accurate detection matters for clinical diagnosis, forensic profiling, structural variant calling, and assembly quality control. They also remain among the most difficult genomic elements to characterize computationally.

The difficulty is that short tandem repeats (STRs, periods 1–6 bp) and satellite DNA (periods 100–2,000+ bp) present fundamentally different detection problems. STRs require identification of near-perfect microsatellite runs, whereas satellites tolerate substantial inter-copy divergence — 20–30% within a CEN180 array (Naish et al., 2021) — and span megabases. Tandem Repeats Finder (TRF; Benson, 1999), the de facto standard for over two decades, uses k-tuple matching followed by wraparound dynamic programming and handles both classes reasonably well, but imposes a maximum detectable period of 2,000 bp and can become prohibitively slow on satellite-rich sequence (Wlodzimierz et al., 2023). In practice the binding constraint is cost rather than that ceiling. Every period-parameterised detector must be told its search range in advance, and what a wide one costs is tool-specific and not predictable from the tool's description: on the maize genome ULTRA took 0.23 h at a maximum period of 6 bp, 12.9 h at 200 bp, and 34.7 h at 500 bp, while TRF over that same span was flat, at 5.2 h, 5.5 h and 5.5 h. Because a user cannot know in advance which case they are in, the safe choice is a narrow range; our own TRF runs were capped at 500 bp on human and 200 bp on Arabidopsis for that reason. A method whose cost is insensitive to the requested range removes the choice and can search the whole span in one pass, which is the design goal pursued here.

Subsequent tools have attacked different parts of the problem. Alignment-free and combinatorial methods such as mreps (Kolpakov et al., 2003) using Hamming distance and tantan (Frith, 2011) using low-complexity masking are fast but limited. mreps does not model indels, and tantan is designed for masking, though the per-call period and motif fields it emits are usable for scoring (Section 3.3.1). Probabilistic approaches such as ULTRA (Olson and Wheeler, 2024) apply HMM-based Viterbi decoding to label sequence as repetitive and report a period and consensus for each labelled region. At the other end, template-guided tools like NCRF (Harris et al., 2019) and satellite-specialized annotators like TRASH (Wlodzimierz et al., 2023) achieve high accuracy for known repeat families, but NCRF requires motifs to be specified a priori and TRASH requires substantial runtime on large genomes. TideHunter (Gao et al., 2019) was designed for noisy long reads rather than assembled genomes.

The completion of telomere-to-telomere (T2T) assemblies for human (Nurk et al., 2022), Arabidopsis thaliana (Naish et al., 2021), and Zea mays (Chen et al., 2023) has resolved centromeric and satellite-rich regions for the first time. Combined with the GIAB consortium's adotto tandem repeat benchmark for human (English et al., 2024), these resources make it possible to evaluate detection tools across the full range of repeat classes on complete genome sequences. One scoping note: the human benchmark below uses GRCh38 rather than a T2T assembly, since the adotto catalog is defined on GRCh38 — the human centromeric satellite the T2T assemblies resolved is therefore outside this comparison, and the T2T evidence here comes from the Arabidopsis and maize genomes.

Compressed indexes are not new to repeat analysis; suffix arrays and the FM-index underpin read mappers and have been used to enumerate exact repeats. To our knowledge, what has not been available is a detector whose cost is both low and predictably insensitive to the period range it is asked to search. TRF and ULTRA take a maximum period, which forces the user to bound the arrays they are trying to find before finding them; for ULTRA that bound is expensive to set generously, while TRF's cost, we find, is nearly flat in it (5.2 h at a 6 bp maximum against 5.5 h at 500 bp on maize; Tables 3A–3C) — but a user cannot know in advance which case a tool is. mreps takes the same kind of argument, though we ran it unbounded on human; tantan defaults to a maximum period of 100 bp, which we left at its default, so it reports nothing above that here; TRASH in de novo mode needs neither a period bound nor a motif but pays in runtime (107.6 hours on human); and the template-guided tools sidestep the question by requiring the motif itself.

Here we present BWTandem, which uses the FM-index to replace exhaustive position-by-position alignment with targeted queries that return the suffix-array interval of a sampled k-mer in time proportional to the k-mer length, and its occurrence positions in time proportional to their number. Sparsely sampled k-mers whose occurrence positions form periodic patterns identify candidate repeat regions directly, and alignment runs only on confirmed candidates, which is intended to avoid the cost alignment-based methods incur on dense satellite arrays; Section 4.3 reports what our data can and cannot establish about that. BWTandem implements a three-tier pipeline: direct character comparison for microsatellites, optionally supplemented by an FM-index enumeration pass over short motifs — the configuration used for every run reported here — then LCP array analysis with BWT k-mer seeding for medium-length repeats, and sparse BWT k-mer seeding with adaptive parameters for long satellite arrays.

Three properties are demonstrated here; the first two follow from that design. First, a single run covers periods of 1 to 2,000 bp on the human genome in 12.1 hours of wall clock, against 29.8 hours for ULTRA restricted to 1–100 bp and 33.7 hours for TRF at a 500 bp maximum: a twentyfold wider search than ULTRA's at 41% of its wall clock. BWTandem and ULTRA ran on two threads and TRF on one, so in core-hours the figures are 24.2, 59.6 and 33.7, and the margin over TRF is narrower than the wall clock suggests. Second, a single wide-range pass needs no per-genome range tuning, which on the Arabidopsis centromere is the difference between recovering 99.7% of CEN180 monomers and recovering 0.6% — the figure the closest competitor returns at its default period ceiling of 100 bp, which cannot represent a 178 bp monomer. That competitor needed only a higher ceiling, not a target length, and Section 3.2 reports what it recovers once given one. Third — a property of an optional periodicity pass layered on the pipeline rather than of the index itself — sensitivity is set at run time, so one build serves four measured operating points spanning 54.7% to 81.6% region recall, and a user chooses among them; a single-parameter identity sweep traces the same trade-off continuously (Supplementary Table S3). We benchmarked on three genomes spanning a twenty-threefold range in size against seven existing tools, six of which produced results we report; TideHunter was excluded for runtime without a completed run.
2. Methods
2.1 BWTandem Algorithm Architecture
2.1.1 Indexing and Data Structures
Given a genomic sequence S of length n, BWTandem constructs a suffix array using divsufsort (Mori, 2008), which is O(n log n) in the worst case and near-linear in practice on genomic input, and derives the Burrows-Wheeler Transform in a single linear pass. An FM-index (Ferragina and Manzini, 2000) is built on these structures. Backward search returns the suffix-array interval of any query of length k in O(k) time, independent of how many times the query occurs; recovering the occ occurrence positions within that interval costs a further O(occ). The seeding strategy below exploits both: the interval is located in time independent of how often the k-mer occurs, and the candidate period is then read off the occurrence list at a cost proportional to its length, with no scan of the sequence. For medium-length repeat detection, the longest common prefix (LCP) array (Kasai et al., 2001) is computed in O(n) time on first use by Tier 2 and cached for the chromosome; it is not built when Tier 2 is disabled. Index construction requires O(n) space.

BWTandem processes each chromosome independently, constructing a single FM-index and then applying a three-tier detection pipeline in sequence. Each tier records detected regions in a coverage mask: Tier 2's general scan and Tier 3 seed only from unmasked positions and reject candidates more than half covered, while Tier 2's long-unit phase scans without the Tier-1 mask and instead discards any call that overlaps a Tier-1 interval.
2.1.2 Tier 1: Short Tandem Repeat Detection (Periods 1–9 bp)
The first tier detects microsatellites with periods of one to nine base pairs through direct character comparison, without using the FM-index. For each candidate period p ∈ {1, …, 9}, a boolean match vector M is constructed where M[i] = 1 iff S[i] = S[i + p]. Consecutive runs of ones identify candidate repeat regions, retained only if the array's length reaches both an absolute floor and the period times a period-dependent minimum copy count, which imposes stricter thresholds on shorter periods. Under the relaxed short-period floors used by three of the four configurations the copy-count minimum binds only at periods six and nine; under BWTandem-P's higher floor it never binds, and at every other period the length floor is the binding constraint (Supplementary Methods S1.1).

Candidates are bidirectionally extended while the cumulative Hamming mismatch fraction remains below 20% — extension is attempted for motifs of three bases or less only when the perfect seed already spans at least two copies (five in the library default) — then refined via banded dynamic programming alignment, which simultaneously reduces the motif to its primitive period and computes consensus statistics. Candidates with quality scores below a minimum threshold are discarded. Periods are processed longest-first; an accepted call claims its seed span, and a shorter-period candidate whose start or midpoint falls in claimed sequence is discarded, so a period-6 array is not also reported at period 2 or 3.

An optional FM-index enumeration mode, enabled for every run reported in this paper, runs as an additive second stage over the sequence the sliding-window scan did not already claim. It queries every primitive motif rotation of each enumerated period length — 1 to 6 bp in this configuration, since rotations are not collapsed to a canonical form — to retrieve their genomic positions. Positions forming exact arithmetic progressions of the motif length — tolerating up to two skipped copies per step — are grouped into candidate arrays, and two separate gates suppress false positives: a density floor (observed occurrences per expected copy) and a Poisson log-likelihood-ratio test of the observed occurrence count against an independent-bases null, with base composition estimated from a subsample of up to two million bases (thresholds in Supplementary Methods S1.1); motifs occurring more than 2×10⁷ times genome-wide are skipped, which bounds the occurrence array materialised for any one motif. The pass applies its own acceptance gate in place of the length, score and copy-count gate above (Supplementary Methods S1.1).
2.1.3 Tier 2: Medium-Length Imperfect Repeat Detection (Periods ≥ 10 bp)
The second tier targets minisatellites and medium-length repeats containing substitutions and indels through two complementary phases.

In the first phase, the LCP array is scanned to identify adjacent suffixes sharing long common prefixes. For each pair where the LCP value is at least the phase threshold (10 here; the 10–50 bp scan's pre-pass uses max(8, min-period/2), which resolves to 8), the positional difference d = |SA[i] − SA[i + 1]| is computed. If d falls within the target period range, it serves as a candidate period seed. Seeds are extended bidirectionally with up to 30% mismatch tolerance in the runs reported here — the library default is 20%, and every run in this paper raised it (Supplementary Methods S2) — reduced to their primitive period via divisibility checks and autocorrelation, and refined through banded dynamic programming alignment.

The second phase applies BWT k-mer seeding to regions the previous phases left more than half uncovered, which is the primary mechanism through which the FM-index drives repeat detection. K-mers of 9 bp are sampled every 5 bp in this phase (the long-unit phase above uses 10 bp k-mers every 10 bp) and queried via backward search in O(k) time. Occurrence positions are retrieved, sorted, and tested for arithmetic progressions with common difference within the target period range (3% jitter tolerance). Progressions with sufficient copies define candidate regions, which are extended bidirectionally and refined as in the first phase. Both sub-scans run an LCP pre-pass followed by k-mer seeding, over different windows: the long-unit scan covers periods of 20 bp up to the requested maximum (2,000 bp here, so it overlaps Tier 3's window), with LCP threshold 10 and 10 bp k-mers at stride 10; the general scan covers 10–50 bp with LCP threshold 8 and 9 bp k-mers at stride 5. Periods of 10–19 bp are therefore reached only by the general scan and periods above 50 bp only by the long-unit scan.

For highly diverged sequences where exact k-mer recurrence fails, an autocorrelation-based mode computes the fraction of positions where S[i] = S[i + p] across sliding windows. Contiguous stretches exceeding a minimum identity threshold (default 78%) are flagged as periodic, recovering arrays too diverged for seed-based detection. This mode is opt-in and was left disabled in every run reported here; on the human benchmark the arrays it recovers lie inside regions the other phases already touch, so it moves region recall little while lowering base pair precision.
2.1.4 Tier 3: Long Satellite Repeat Detection (Periods 100 bp to 100 kbp)
The third tier detects long satellite arrays and centromeric repeats using the same BWT k-mer seeding procedure as Tier 2, with sparser sampling and longer k-mers suited to large period scales. The tier's search window is fixed at 100 bp to 100 kbp; the command-line maximum period filters its report rather than bounding its search, a distinction Section 3.1's range-cost measurement depends on.

The tier automatically adapts its parameters based on input sequence properties. K-mer size scales logarithmically with sequence length (k = ⌊10 + 6 · log₁₀(max(n / 10⁵, 1))⌋ under the balanced preset used here; the fast and sensitive presets shift it by ±2), sampling stride scales proportionally to n / 40,000, and the maximum allowed k-mer occurrence count is set to n / 30,000 to filter low-complexity k-mers. GC content modulates the mismatch tolerance. When prior tier coverage exceeds 50%, the stride is reduced to scan uncovered regions more densely. Sequences above 100 Mb first take additional fixed caps, and all adaptive parameters are then clamped to empirically validated ranges (Supplementary Methods S1.3). The parameters are computed per chromosome, so the realized values vary with chromosome length and composition, not genome: the stride saturates at 300 on every chromosome above roughly 24 Mb (and on the smaller Arabidopsis chromosomes unless prior-tier coverage exceeds 90%), the mismatch tolerance spans roughly 15–16% with each chromosome's GC, but the k-mer size runs from 24 on the smallest Arabidopsis chromosome to the 28 bp ceiling on chromosomes above 100 Mb, and the occurrence cap is 500 above 100 Mb while formula-driven below it — roughly 730–1,090 on the Arabidopsis chromosomes and up to the 1,500 ceiling on mid-sized human chromosomes, so a 90 Mb chromosome receives three times the occurrence budget of a 250 Mb one. Supplementary Methods S1.3 tabulates the realized values; the coverage-dependent stride reduction has no effect on sequences above roughly 24 Mb, because the clamp binds before it does.

Boundary refinement uses two strategies depending on array scale. For large arrays (more than 10 kb or more than 100 copies), an anchor-based strategy scans backward up to 20–80 and forward up to 200–800 period-length increments (chromosome-length dependent; Supplementary Methods S1.3), terminating when the match fraction drops below a threshold of 70–80%. This linear-time approach avoids the quadratic dynamic programming costs that megabase arrays would otherwise incur. For smaller arrays, banded Needleman-Wunsch alignment determines precise boundaries.
2.1.5 Post-Processing and Supplementary Detection Modes
Post-processing consolidates raw detections by sorting coordinates, merging adjacent calls of the same motif family, and resolving overlaps. Five constants govern this, and we state them because they act directly on the coverage and base pair metrics reported below. First, the merge gap is period-dependent: max(10, p) bp for periods below 20, 10p for periods of 20–99, and 100p for periods of 100 and above, so two calls of a 178 bp monomer separated by as much as 17.8 kb of unclaimed sequence can be merged into a single interval. Second, merging normally requires the same canonical motif — the lexicographically smallest rotation among all cyclic permutations of the forward and reverse complement — but for motifs of 50 bp and longer, motifs of equal length within 10% Hamming distance are treated as one family. Third, overlap resolution retains the higher-scoring call only when the overlap exceeds half the shorter interval, comparing each call against the previously retained one in coordinate order — pairwise along the sorted list, not globally; smaller overlaps leave both calls in the output. Fourth and fifth, a merge across a gap must also pass a content gate: for periods of 100 bp and above with a gap longer than the period, the intervening sequence must be at least twice the motif length and show autocorrelation identity of at least 0.55 at the motif period — the condition the 17.8 kb monomer merge above is subject to; a gap longer than the period but shorter than twice it fails the gate and the calls stay separate — and otherwise a trial re-scan of the merged span must keep the mismatch fraction within max(twice the calls' mean, 0.15). The order of operations is merge, then overlap resolution, then the two supplementary passes below, each followed by a re-merge — the gap-fill runs up to twice and the catch-all last — so overlap resolution never sees the supplementary calls.

Two supplementary passes address gaps in the core pipeline. A satellite gap-filling mechanism examines uncovered blocks flanking detected satellite arrays (motif of at least 100 bp) and evaluates autocorrelation identity at candidate periods between 100 and 360 bp (Supplementary Methods S1.4). Only uncovered blocks of 300 bp to 100 kb lying within 50 kb of such an anchor are considered, and identity is sampled from up to three 5 kb windows inside the block rather than from the whole of it. If the best identity found exceeds approximately 45% — the search stopping early at the first period exceeding 0.80 — — against the ≈0.26 expected for independent bases at these genomes' composition, and a measured median of 0.268 in maize intergenic sequence (Section 3.3.3) — the entire block is emitted as one call, taking its motif from the sampled window and a mismatch rate of one minus the measured identity, so a call accepted at the floor is reported at 55% divergence. This recovers interior gaps within highly diverged centromeric arrays that retain periodic signal but are too diverged for exact k-mer seeding.

An optional catch-all periodicity sweep evaluates windowed autocorrelation at periods 1–20 bp across uncovered sequence in O(n) time, using a window of twice the candidate period subject to a 12 bp floor. Stretches of at least 20 bp exceeding a minimum identity threshold (default 72%) and spanning a minimum copy count (length divided by period; default two, three in the human and maize runs) are reported as additional detections, with the reported interval running to the end of the last passing window, so a call extends up to one window past the last position tested; candidates already more than 30% covered by an existing call are dropped. Periods are evaluated longest-first and an accepted call excludes its span from shorter periods. This mode — the catch-all periodicity pass, the name used throughout — emphasizes STR recall and is disabled by default, so an unconfigured run reproduces none of the human or maize headline numbers (Supplementary Methods S2).
2.2 Benchmarking Methodology
BWTandem was benchmarked against seven tools: TRF 4.10.0rc2 (Benson, 1999), mreps 2.6.01 (Kolpakov et al., 2003), ULTRA 1.2.1 (Olson and Wheeler, 2024), tantan 51 (Frith, 2011), NCRF 1.01.02 (Harris et al., 2019), TRASH v1 (Wlodzimierz et al., 2023), and TideHunter 1.5.6 (Gao et al., 2019). Every competitor run was executed inside one Singularity image; versions and full command lines are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Six of the seven produced results we report. TideHunter produced no completed run on any of the three genomes and is absent from every table; the runs were abandoned for runtime, but we did not record the wall clock at which, so we can state the exclusion without quantifying it. mreps was excluded from the maize experiments for runtime and did not complete on human (Table 1a). NCRF was excluded on human for memory: it required 80.96 GB on the 132 Mb Arabidopsis assembly (Table 2), and no completed human run exists. TRASH was evaluated in both de novo and template modes on Arabidopsis and maize; on human only the de novo mode was run, so the single human TRASH row is a de novo result. Motif-guided tools were provided exact consensus sequences.

Period search ranges were not uniform across tools, and because this affects which metrics are comparable we state them explicitly. BWTandem was run at 1–2,000 bp on every genome. TRF has no minimum-period parameter and was run to a maximum of 500 bp on human, 200 bp on Arabidopsis, and the maximum matching each maize experiment. ULTRA was run at its default maximum period of 100 bp on human and at 6, 500, and 200 bp for maize experiments 3A, 3B, and 3C; the maize and Arabidopsis-re-run invocations additionally set --read_all, -i 3 and -d 3, which the human run did not (Supplementary Table S1). tantan was run at its default maximum period of 100 bp on every genome; that ceiling is tunable, and we did not test a wider one. mreps was run without period bounds on human. On Arabidopsis, ULTRA and mreps at their original settings could not represent a 178 bp monomer — ULTRA at a 100 bp maximum, mreps at 6 bp — so both were re-run, at a 500 bp maximum and over 150–400 bp respectively, and Table 2 reports the re-runs. TRF's cost later proved nearly flat in its maximum period (Tables 3A–3C), so the 500 bp human cap — set before that was measured — could likely have been lifted at little cost; the run was not repeated, and the above-100 stratum of Table 1c is unmatched in consequence. Search ranges therefore differ by up to twentyfold on human and by more than three hundredfold across the maize experiments. Region-level metrics are affected only marginally, but base pair metrics computed over the full range are not directly comparable between tools, since 33.4% of BWTandem's called bases and 65.7% of TRF's lie above period 100, on the merged-interval basis of Tables 1a and 1c, while ULTRA and tantan report nothing there. Section 3.1 therefore reports a matched-range comparison restricted to periods ≤100 bp, the widest range every tool could enter, alongside the full-range figures and a separate stratum for periods 101–2,000 bp.
2.2.1 Computational Environment and Execution
All benchmarking was performed on the University of Nevada, Reno Pronghorn HPC cluster using Intel Xeon E5-2683 v4 processors at 2.10 GHz, with each genome processed as an independent SLURM job. Allocations differed by tool: the BWTandem runs behind Tables 1a, 1c, 2 and 3 used two cores, the matched-range runs behind Tables 1b and 1d used four, TRF, mreps and tantan one, and the remaining tools two. Cost figures fall into two provenance classes and the distinction should be read into every runtime and memory cell below. Every BWTandem figure was re-measured from a single recorded execution and is traceable in the provenance manifest to a job identifier, an elapsed time and a cgroup peak. The competitor figures are, with two exceptions, inherited from an earlier benchmarking round whose accounting records no longer exist; the output files and command lines survive, the job entries do not, so those cells carry the wall clock and maximum resident set size that GNU time reported at the time and cannot be re-derived. The exceptions are the ULTRA and mreps Arabidopsis re-runs, which have job records and carry accounting values like the BWTandem rows. We report them because they are the best record available and because the accuracy figures they accompany do reproduce, but competitor cost should be read as approximate and BWTandem cost as documented.

BWTandem was configured for the full period range of 1 to 2,000 bp using two threads for the whole-genome comparisons of Tables 1a, 2 and 3, and four threads for the matched-range operating-point runs behind Table 1b, Table 1d and Figure 1. Call count is identical across thread counts, verified at one, two and four threads (4,009,261 calls in all three); the outputs are not byte-identical across thread counts — the deposited files' leading-megabyte hashes differ, consistent with record order following worker completion — so the invariance verified is of call count and content, not bytes. Thread count changes both cost axes. At the full range on human, a one-thread run took 15 h 12 m at 13.32 GB and a two-thread run 9 h 21 m at 21.38 GB, against 12.1 h at 21.86 GB for the separately executed two-thread run reported in Table 1a; the two two-thread runs differ by 29% in wall clock at an identical call count, which sets the scale of run-to-run variation on this cluster and is a further reason to read the cost tables as approximate. Memory fits a fixed base of about 5 GB plus roughly 8 GB per thread (--threads spawns worker processes; one FM-index is held per in-flight chromosome); the four-thread run measured 35.28 GB against a two-point extrapolation of 37.5 GB, so the scaling is mildly sublinear. Tier 1 used FM-index enumeration mode. The catch-all periodicity pass was enabled for the human and Zea mays genomes with a minimum autocorrelation of 72% and minimum 3 copies, but disabled for Arabidopsis to preserve specificity. Tier 3 used the balanced preset (--tier3-mode balanced, the default) with adaptive parameters. The BWTandem configurations override library defaults throughout (Supplementary Methods S2); a reader running the tool unconfigured will not reproduce these numbers. In the whole-genome cost tables (Tables 1a, 2 and 3A–3C) the multithreaded tools, BWTandem and ULTRA among them, were constrained to two threads; Table 1d's runtime column instead carries the four-thread matched-range runs and its caption says so. The single-threaded tools were run as single processes; mreps was applied to one sequence at a time in sequence. Runtime for the BWTandem rows is the SLURM accounting elapsed time, corroborated by GNU time where both were recorded; competitor runtime is GNU time wall clock. Peak memory for the BWTandem rows is the SLURM cgroup maximum (sacct MaxRSS), because BWTandem distributes chromosomes across worker processes and GNU time reports the maximum over children rather than their sum, which undercounts a multi-worker run. The competing tools are single processes, so their rows carry the GNU time maximum resident set size, which for them is the same quantity. The human runs predate a fix that stops the satellite gap-filling pass from emitting motifs containing characters other than A, C, G and T; a second released fix renders the column recording each call's originating pass numerically, where the deposited outputs carry a text label on satellite calls — a formatting change that alters bytes but no coordinate, motif or statistic. The human output holds 235 such calls spanning 4.81 Mb; 198 of them, spanning 4.57 Mb, come from the gap-filling pass and are removed by the released code, while the remaining 37, spanning 0.24 Mb, originate in Tiers 1 to 3, which that fix does not guard. Removing the 198 raises base pair precision by roughly 0.4 percentage points, so the figures reported here are the conservative ones. The Arabidopsis and maize runs contain no such calls and are unaffected.
2.2.2 Genome Assemblies
Performance was assessed across three genome assemblies representing distinct scales and repeat landscapes. The human GRCh38 reference genome (3.1 Gb) served as the primary benchmark target. The two sides of that comparison used different files, and we state the difference because it bears on the cost figures. The competitors were run on the GenBank FASTA for accession GCA_000001405.15, which carries unplaced scaffolds and alt loci in addition to the chromosomes; BWTandem was run on the 24 primary chromosome sequences of the UCSC hg38 release of the same assembly (chr1–22, X, Y; a 3.15 GB FASTA). Scoring restricts both sides to those 24 chromosomes — competitor outputs were first converted to UCSC chromosome names via the accession map in the deposited conversion script, and everything unmapped is dropped — so no accuracy figure is affected; tantan, for example, makes 149,706 of its calls outside them, and all are discarded before scoring. The competitors' runtime and memory do, however, cover roughly 5% more sequence than BWTandem's. The telomere to telomere Arabidopsis thaliana Col-CEN v1.2 assembly (132 Mb, Naish et al., 2021) provided a compact genome with well characterized centromeric satellite arrays; the assembly is distributed at https://github.com/schatzlab/Col-CEN, and the reads it was built from are in ArrayExpress under E-MTAB-10272 (Oxford Nanopore) and the European Nucleotide Archive under PRJEB46164 (PacBio HiFi). The Zea mays Mo17 T2T assembly (2.18 Gb, Chen et al., 2023, GenBank accession GCA_022117705.1) served as a large, repeat rich crop genome with multiple annotated satellite families.
2.2.3 Evaluation Metrics
For the human genome (Experiment 1), accuracy was evaluated against release v1.2.1 of the GIAB adotto tandem repeat region catalog (English et al., 2024; https://zenodo.org/records/13987414), restricted to chr1–22, X and Y. Region-level recall and precision were defined as the fraction of benchmark or tool-predicted regions overlapping at least one counterpart by at least one base pair (`bedtools intersect -u`); no reciprocal-overlap fraction was required. That criterion is generous to tools reporting few long intervals and should be read alongside the call counts, which differ by three orders of magnitude across the tools compared, and alongside the period-dependent merge described in Section 2.1.5, under which one BWTandem interval can satisfy several benchmark regions. Base pair recall and precision were computed on merged intervals and are not subject to that effect. Concretely, tool intervals are merged with bedtools; base pair recall is the summed length of intersections between benchmark regions and merged tool intervals divided by total benchmark bases, and base pair precision the same intersection divided by total merged tool bases. The benchmark is a region catalog — merged intervals around repeat loci — rather than a base-resolution repeat annotation, so base pair metrics against it inherit its interval conventions. Period filters for the matched-range and stratum panels use the tool-reported period column, falling back to motif length where a tool reports none. Because the GIAB catalog is known to be incomplete, we computed an adjusted precision in which predictions not overlapping any benchmark region were tested for corroboration by ULTRA and tantan — a prediction counts as corroborated if it overlaps any corroborator call by at least one base pair — and corroborated predictions were reclassified as true positives. mreps is not scored on this genome and acts as corroborator in neither rule. A tool cannot corroborate its own calls, so the ULTRA and tantan rows have one corroborator each while the remaining rows have two; adjusted precision is consequently not directly comparable across rows of Tables 1a and 1e, and we also report a symmetric leave-one-out variant in which every other tool acts as a corroborator. Neither corroborator searched above period 100, so neither can corroborate a call above it; we verified that this biases the figure by only +0.11 percentage points — recomputing BWTandem's adjusted precision with the above-100 stratum excluded moves it from 79.49% to 79.60% — since such calls are 2.5% of the total, and the bias runs against BWTandem rather than for it. The number of unique regions detected exclusively by each tool was also recorded.

For Arabidopsis thaliana (Experiment 2), performance was measured as mean centromere coverage across five centromeric regions (Naish et al., 2021), computed with no period filter. CEN180 monomer recall was computed against reference monomer coordinates obtained by aligning a 177 bp consensus to the Col-CEN v1.2 assembly with blastn. We use the family's common name CEN180 in column headers; the Col-CEN literature names the family CEN178 after its 178 bp modal monomer length, and the consensus we aligned is 177 bp — three numbers, one family. The blastn version and command line were not retained, so we deposit the inputs and outputs rather than the invocation: the consensus sequence, the 68,840 raw hits, and the 66,683 monomers that survive filtering, which span 175–179 bp at 80–100% identity. Monomer recall is therefore measured over this conserved subset; monomers diverged below 80% identity from the consensus are outside the truth set. Every row of Table 2 is scored against that one coordinate set, so no comparison in this paper depends on the lost command; a reader regenerating the coordinates from scratch would, however, have to choose their own parameters and should expect a slightly different set. The two CEN180 columns of Table 2 use different call sets, applied identically to every row: monomer recall accepts a call of any reported period, while the CEN180 count admits only calls of reported period 150–200 bp. The banded rule is period-assignment sensitive — a tool that calls the array as its ~356 bp dimer covers it perfectly and still scores zero — so Section 3.2 reports monomer recall under the unfiltered rule, the 150–200 bp band, and a 150–400 bp band that admits the dimer.

For Zea mays (Experiment 3), three aspects were evaluated. Experiment 3A measured microsatellite detection (periods ≤6 bp), with TAG arrays as diagnostic markers of heterochromatic knob repeats. Two scoring rules are in play here and both are reported. The rule used in our earlier analysis applied the 1–6 bp period band to the BWTandem row alone, scoring every other tool on its whole output, and counted a TAG array as any call whose motif column contains the substring TAG or CTA; Table 3A retains it so the two are comparable. Table 3A-b applies the band to every tool and counts a TAG array as a call of period 3 whose leading trinucleotide is a rotation of TAG. Table 3A-b is the rule we would ask a reader to use. Experiment 3B evaluated satellite detection against curated coordinates for 25 knob180 (~180 bp) and 17 TR-1 (~358 bp) arrays from Chen et al. (2023). Metrics included detection sensitivity, mean boundary offset error, and a normalized fragmentation score (1.0 = single contiguous call per array). Experiment 3C assessed 17 CentC centromeric arrays (~156 bp) using the same metrics plus centromere coverage. mreps was excluded from maize experiments; NCRF was included only in Experiment 3A.

Every figure in this paper is a single-run point estimate, and we report no confidence intervals or significance tests. Each tool is deterministic given its inputs (mreps excepted, since it randomizes assembly-gap bases; and our own multi-worker output order varies while call content does not), so the estimates carry no sampling error in the usual sense; what they lack is any estimate of how much a difference between two tools would move under a different benchmark, a different chromosome set, or a different curation of the ground truth. The differences we build arguments on are large — a twentyfold range difference in cost, a 49-point region-recall gap between BWTandem and TRF on the matched range (Table 1b), the presence or absence of any call above period 100. Several differences reported below are not: the three-way spread in Arabidopsis centromere coverage is 0.20 percentage points, the CentC coverage lead is 0.35, and the gap between BWTandem's most sensitive setting and ULTRA on region recall is 0.02. We describe those as ties rather than as rankings, and no claim in the Discussion rests on one. The provenance manifest names the scoring script and its hash for every table, so a reader wishing to resample the ground truth can rerun the same code.
3. Results
3.1 Tandem Repeat Detection on Human GRCh38
Six tools were run on the 3.1 Gb human GRCh38 genome against the GIAB adotto benchmark; five produced scorable output (Table 1a). TideHunter was excluded due to excessive runtime, and NCRF was excluded due to excessive memory consumption.
 
 The cost of searching a wide period range was measured directly, by running BWTandem twice on the same genome, same code and same thread count, changing only the ceiling. Both runs used four threads. One qualification applies: the ceiling bounds Tiers 1 and 2 and filters the report, but Tier 3's internal search window is fixed at 100 bp–100 kb regardless of it (Section 2.1.4), so the narrow arm performed the same long-period search and discarded its findings — the ratio measures the cost of reporting a wider range under this architecture, not of a search that was itself narrowed. Searching 1–2,000 bp took 7 h 23 m against 5 h 17 m for 1–100 bp: a twentyfold wider search for 1.40 times the runtime and no additional memory (35.28 against 35.24 GB). Both arms are single executions; the 29% same-configuration run-to-run spread reported in Section 2.2.1 bounds the ratio's precision, and the memory pair is the tighter observation. These are not the numbers in Table 1a, which reports the two-thread run of the same configuration at 12.1 h; the range-cost comparison needs a fixed thread count and the cross-tool comparison needs the two-thread allocation, so they are different executions of identical settings and both are recorded in the manifest. ULTRA pays more than linearly by comparison — on maize it goes from 0.23 h at a maximum period of 6 bp to 34.7 h at 500 bp, an 83-fold range increase (ratio of maximum periods) for a 151-fold cost increase — though that pair comes from a different genome and thread allocation than ours, so the two are indicative rather than matched. Seeding candidate positions once from the index and extending only confirmed candidates is what decouples the two, and it is why BWTandem can be run at its full range by default rather than at a range the user has to guess. The index depends only on the sequence and not on any detection setting, so a sensitivity sweep can reuse it rather than rebuilding it per point; an on-disk cache loads a stored index in 0.6 s where construction takes 9 s on the Arabidopsis assembly and 21 minutes across the 24 human chromosomes. Construction is 0.5% of a Col-CEN run (9 s against 31 minutes) and 2.9 to 7.8% of a human one (21 minutes against the 12.1 h full-range and 4.5 h fastest matched-range runs), so reuse is a convenience for sweeps rather than a material part of the cost, and the cost figures reported here are for runs that each built their own index. Against the competitors on this genome, it searched 1–2,000 bp in 12.1 hours while ULTRA took 29.8 hours restricted to 1–100 bp and TRF 33.7 hours at a 500 bp maximum.

	 Within the range all tools shared, periods of 100 bp or less, the three high-recall methods are close and ULTRA is marginally ahead: region recall 81.62% for ULTRA, 79.88% for BWTandem, 78.00% for tantan, with TRF at 30.80% (Table 1b). ULTRA also leads base pair recall at 38.14% against BWTandem's 32.72%. Above period 100 the field thins to three: ULTRA and tantan, run at their default 100 bp ceilings, report nothing there, and of the three that do, BWTandem recovers the most — 3.44% of benchmark regions and 13.44% of benchmark bases in that stratum at 65.97% region precision, in the same single pass (Table 1c). TRF reaches 2.02% and 11.83% there, more precisely but after a 33.7 hour run capped at 500 bp — its stratum therefore ends at 500 bp where BWTandem's runs to 2,000, so the two are not range-matched (Table 1c) — and TRASH 0.25% and 5.23%. The full-range base pair figures — 43.42% for BWTandem against ULTRA's 38.14% — combine both strata and are reported for completeness only; they measure search range as much as sensitivity. mreps did not complete on this assembly and is excluded (Table 1a).

	 BWTandem does not occupy one point in this space. Its sensitivity is set by an optional periodicity pass with a tunable identity threshold, so a user selects an operating point at run time, and we report the whole curve (Figure 1, Table 1d). Region recall spans 54.69% to 81.60% and region precision 62.17% down to 48.43%. At the conservative end BWTandem-P reaches 62.17% region precision, the highest of any method recovering more than half the benchmark: tantan manages 57.66% and ULTRA 53.66%, and the only tools above it, TRF and TRASH, recover 30.80% and 0.91% of the catalog respectively. At the aggressive end BWTandem-H matches ULTRA's region recall to 0.02 percentage points, at 5.2 points lower precision, and the intermediate settings sit between. These four configurations differ in more than the threshold — P both disables the pass and omits the relaxed short-period gate, B raises the swept-period ceiling from its default 20 bp to 50 bp, and F additionally requires three copies (Supplementary Methods S2) — so Table 1d is not a single-parameter sweep. A true sweep, holding every other setting fixed and moving only the identity threshold, moves region recall from 62.59% with the pass disabled to 81.46% at identity 0.68, and region precision from 54.77% down to 48.94% (Supplementary Table S3). That sweep runs over the full 1–2,000 bp range and is scored by a different script from Table 1d, so its absolute values are not directly comparable with the matched-range panel; the shape is what it establishes. The response is not linear in the threshold: identities 0.80 and 0.76 land within 0.2 points of each other on both axes, and the largest single step, 9.0 of the 18.9 points of recall the sweep spans, arrives between 0.76 and 0.72. In base pair space ULTRA leads on recall at 38.14% against BWTandem's best of 33.57%, while BWTandem-P is more precise than ULTRA at 62.56% against 61.33%, though tantan at 70.22% and TRF at 93.16% are more precise still on far smaller outputs; the aggressive settings sit well below both. We did not sweep the competitors' own sensitivity parameters, so the asymmetry between a curve and a set of points reflects how the comparison was run rather than a property the other tools lack.

	 The periodicity pass is what converts precision into recall, and its cost falls almost entirely on base pair precision: 62.56% with the pass disabled against 34–35% with it enabled at any threshold, for a 6.8 point gain in base pair recall. Classifying every called base by its distance to the nearest benchmark region shows where those bases go. Bases lying outside a benchmark region but within 500 bp of one — the ones a boundary trim would remove — are 13.8% of BWTandem's output with the pass enabled, the lowest of the three tools for which we ran this decomposition and below ULTRA's 19.7% and tantan's 14.8%; TRF and TRASH were not decomposed. Bases more than 500 bp from any region rise from 20.1% with the pass disabled — close to ULTRA's 19.0%, above tantan's 15.0% — to 51.7% with it enabled. The pass therefore does not extend existing calls; it makes new ones, in sequence the catalog does not cover. Of the 123.7 Mb it adds, 96.3 Mb falls in that far class, and ULTRA and tantan together report only 40.1 Mb of far sequence in total, so most of it is called by BWTandem and by neither of the two tools we decomposed; TRF and TRASH were not measured this way. The pass is disabled by default for this reason.

	 Adjusted precision, which credits predictions corroborated by an independent tool, is insensitive to this choice: it peaks at the third of the four settings, 79.83%, and varies by 2.7 points across settings whose raw precision spans 13.7 points, because the calls added at lower thresholds are disproportionately ones ULTRA or tantan also report. Under the same rule tantan reaches 82.93% and ULTRA 78.44% (Table 1e; those two rows have one corroborator where BWTandem's has two, so cross-tool comparison is better read from Table 1e's leave-one-out column). On the full-range run, 58.5% of BWTandem's predictions outside the adotto catalog are corroborated by ULTRA or tantan, which places much of the apparent precision gap in catalog incompleteness rather than in false discovery. Section 4.2 shows that under the symmetric leave-one-out rule the same measurement is higher for ULTRA and tantan than for BWTandem, so this does not distinguish the tools in BWTandem's favour.

  BWTandem identified more unique regions — regions not called by any of the four other tools in that comparison — than any of them, roughly 1.7 times the count unique to tantan or ULTRA (full-range run; uniqueness is computed over the unfiltered files, and a larger output has more opportunities to be unique).
   
   BWTandem's runtime of 12.1 hours came with a memory cost. Its FM index required more memory than any other tool run on this genome, 15 times TRF's and 80 times tantan's; larger footprints appear elsewhere in this paper, from NCRF on Arabidopsis and TRASH on maize. tantan finished fastest, at 23.54% base pair recall against BWTandem's 43.42% — full-range figures at unequal search ranges; the matched-range pair is 23.54% against 32.72% — though the two are within 2.6 points at region level. mreps' partial run covered 7 of 24 chromosomes in a comparable wall clock, so per base it was several times slower, and it did not complete; no recall figure exists for it.

   3.2 Centromeric Satellite Detection in Arabidopsis thaliana
   The Arabidopsis thaliana Col CEN v1.2 assembly (132 Mb) provided a particularly informative test case because its five centromeres are dominated by well characterized CEN180 satellite arrays (Table 2).
    
	BWTandem processed this 132 Mb assembly in 31 minutes searching 1–2,000 bp. TRF, capped at 200 bp, required 131.9 hours — more than 250 times BWTandem's 31 minutes, on a genome an order of magnitude smaller than human, where the same tool took 33.7 hours. ULTRA needed 10.1 hours once raised to a 500 bp maximum, 20 times BWTandem's single pass. Runtime on satellite-dense sequence is where the index-based approach separates from the alignment-based and model-based tools that detect the arrays — tantan (0.04 h) and mreps (0.12 h) are faster still but do not detect them at their settings — and among the detecting tools the runtime spread is far larger than the accuracy spread.

	 On accuracy the tools that detect the arrays converge to the point where the differences are not interpretable. Centromere coverage spans 0.20 percentage points across BWTandem (84.59%), ULTRA (84.44%), and TRF (84.39%), with TRASH at 85.03% and NCRF at 85.17%; we read the first three as a tie. At the monomer level BWTandem recovers 99.73% of BLAST-derived CEN180 monomers and ULTRA 99.80%, matching NCRF at 99.63%, which was given the consensus, and TRASH at 99.69%, which reaches the same figure in de novo mode without one. BWTandem's CEN180 base pair precision, 57.17%, is the lowest of the tools that detect the arrays at all; the re-run mreps reaches 98.33%. On this genome the claim is speed, not detection.

	 The comparison required re-running two tools, and the asymmetry is worth stating. At their original settings ULTRA and mreps returned 2.25% and 1.58% centromere coverage — figures forced by a maximum period of 100 bp and 6 bp respectively, neither of which can represent a 178 bp monomer. Raising ULTRA's maximum to 500 bp and running mreps over 150–400 bp lifted them to 84.44% and 58.48%. Only mreps needed a bracketing window; ULTRA needed only a higher ceiling, and was not told where to look. TRF was left at its original 200 bp maximum, which can hold the monomer but not the ~356 bp dimer. We did not re-run the tools that scored well, so the re-runs are asymmetric in BWTandem's disfavour on accuracy and in its favour on nothing.

	 Reported period is a property of the caller as much as of the array, and it moves these numbers more than detection does. Restricting calls to periods of 150–200 bp drops ULTRA to 73.36% monomer recall and BWTandem to 91.92%, while TRF is unchanged at 97.55%; widening to 150–400 bp returns ULTRA to 99.79% and BWTandem to 95.21%, with TRF band-invariant at 97.55% since it assigns the monomer period. ULTRA calls CEN180 predominantly as its approximately 356 bp dimer, BWTandem does so for a minority of arrays, and TRF resolves the monomer. Any analysis that selects calls by an expected monomer length therefore measures period assignment alongside detection.

	 The satellite gap-fill pass contributes materially to these figures, and its contribution is not an artefact of its default period window. The pass closes uncovered gaps between satellite calls when the gap sequence is periodic, using a window of 100–360 bp chosen for the human alpha-satellite monomer and its dimer — a window that also brackets CEN180 at 178 and 356 bp. The ablation was run against a defaults configuration rather than the Table 2 row, and the three arms are therefore internally comparable but offset from the published numbers: the base scores 84.77% coverage and 99.76% monomer recall, against 84.59% and 99.73% in Table 2. Relative to that base, disabling the pass costs 5.82 percentage points of centromere coverage and 6.37 points of monomer recall — absolute values 78.95% and 93.39%, which would rank last among the tools that detect the arrays — so the headline figure, and the three-way coverage tie above, are pipeline-plus-gap-fill results rather than three-tier ones. Moving the window to 400–1,000 bp, which cannot contain either 178 or 356, costs 0.54 and 0.47 points. The mechanism is therefore periodicity at multiples of the monomer rather than a constant tuned to this satellite.

		Every tool in Table 3C detected all 17 CentC centromeric arrays, tantan included, though tantan covers only 1.10% of them. TRF and ULTRA place boundaries most tightly, 70 bp and 58 bp from curated coordinates, with BWTandem at 196 bp and TRASH at 5.3 kb. Across the three maize classes BWTandem is therefore at or near the top on coverage in all three, and does so at a boundary offset under 1 kb throughout — less than half TRF's on knob180, though above TRF's on TR-1 and CentC, and above ULTRA's on all three classes.

		 One caveat applies across these classes and is specific to BWTandem. Filtering calls by reported period, as an analysis targeting a known monomer length would, costs it 15.6, 33.0, and 17.8 percentage points of coverage on knob180, TR-1, and CentC, against at most 1.5 points for TRF and 2.8 for ULTRA across the same three classes (Tables 3B-b and 3C-b). It covers the arrays but frequently reports a period outside the nominal monomer band, so selecting calls by expected monomer length discards correct detections. Users filtering on period should widen the window accordingly.

		 Where BWTandem's calls extend past the curated coordinates, that extension is periodic rather than spurious. Fourteen of the 59 arrays have calls reaching more than 500 bp beyond the curated interval, eleven of them TR-1; measuring windowed autocorrelation identity at the family period across those flanks gives a median of 0.430, against 0.268 for intergenic sequence sampled on the same chromosomes and interior medians of 0.367, 0.329, and 0.405 for knob180, TR-1, and CentC. Thirteen of the fourteen exceed the 95th percentile of background (identity 0.282, from 400 sampled intergenic windows), and 72.7% of the TR-1 flanks are at least as periodic as TR-1 array interiors. One selection effect qualifies this: the fourteen flanks are the ones the detector chose to extend into, so the comparison with unselected background cannot fully distinguish conservative curation from selective extension — the flank medians exceeding the array-interior medians is consistent with either reading — and the matched control on unextended boundaries was not run. With that qualification, the curated coordinates appear conservative at these loci, and a boundary trimming step would remove sequence carrying the family period. The remaining 45 arrays have no flank of this size to measure, which is the same fact the sub-kilobase mean offsets report.

It does establish that over-calling in this band is general to the tools that detect the arrays at all, TRF included despite its otherwise strong boundary precision, and that tantan is the exception on both counts.

		 4. Discussion
		 4.1 Practical significance for de novo genome annotation
		 NCRF requires a curated motif library to exist before it can be run at all; the de novo detectors do not, but they do require the user to bound the period range in advance, which for an uncharacterised satellite is a related problem — and for some of them that bound is expensive to set generously. BWTandem also takes a maximum period; what distinguishes it is that its cost is nearly flat in that parameter, so the bound can sit at the top of the plausible range by default, which is what moves repeat discovery earlier in the annotation process. Candidate arrays can be located directly from an assembly before any species specific library has been built — grouping them into families is a further step this work does not evaluate. The three genomes benchmarked here are among the minority that already have curated satellite coordinates, which is why they can serve as ground truth at all: adotto for human (English et al., 2024), CEN180 for Arabidopsis (Naish et al., 2021), and the knob180, TR-1 and CentC arrays for maize (Chen et al., 2023). That is the situation a tool of this kind is not built for. The case for it rests on the assemblies being completed without such catalogs, and how large that set is relative to the annotated one is something we assert rather than measure.
		 4.2 Why recall over precision
		 A case can be made for favouring recall in first-pass annotation of an uncharacterised genome, which is a different task from annotating a final structural variant call set. A missed array may never enter downstream analyses unless detection is repeated under different assumptions, while a spurious call is one candidate among many that filtering, orthogonal evidence, or curation can remove. The adotto benchmark is itself incomplete, so a tool biased toward completeness has some chance of surfacing catalog gaps rather than only reproducing an existing standard.

		 That argument does not license the operating points at the aggressive end of our curve, and we do not use it that way. A recall-precision trade-off is only a trade if something is gained: BWTandem-H reaches ULTRA's region recall to within 0.02 percentage points but at 5.2 points lower precision, and BWTandem-F trails ULTRA on both axes, so at those settings nothing is being bought. The same holds on Arabidopsis, where ULTRA is level on monomer recall (0.07 points) and ahead on base pair precision. The trade is real only at the conservative end, where disabling the periodicity pass gives the highest raw region precision of any method here that recovers more than half the benchmark — under the published corroboration rule that ordering reverses, BWTandem-P 77.10% against ULTRA's 78.44% and tantan's 82.93% (Table 1d) — and on the period range above 100 bp, which only TRF and TRASH also entered and where BWTandem recovers 3.44% of the catalog against their 2.02% and 0.25%, TRF's stratum ending at its 500 bp cap (Table 1c). Those are the results the recall argument supports; the rest of the curve is offered as a choice a user can make, not as a claim that the choice is favourable.

		 The corroboration analysis does not settle this either, and it does not run in BWTandem's favour. Under the published rule, 58.5% of BWTandem's out-of-catalog predictions are also called by ULTRA or tantan, against 53.5% and 59.7% for those two — but that rule gives BWTandem two corroborators and each of them only one, so the comparison is not like-for-like. Under the leave-one-out rule, where every tool has the same four corroborators, BWTandem's figure is essentially unchanged at 59.2% while ULTRA's and tantan's rise to roughly 71.5% and 71.6%. BWTandem's out-of-catalog calls are therefore the least corroborated of the three, not the most. Catalog incompleteness raises all of these figures rather than closing the gap between them, and in any case the corroborating tools are de novo detectors responding to the same periodicity signal, not independent evidence.
		 4.3 Comparison with existing approaches
		 Tandem repeats have a structural property that an index reaches more directly than a scan. A sampled kmer drawn from within a repeat array recurs at every copy, so the occurrence positions falling inside that array form an approximate arithmetic progression whose common difference is the period of the array itself. Periodicity can therefore be read directly from a sorted list of occurrence positions before any alignment takes place. Alignment based methods reach the same signal less directly. TRF, for instance, also derives candidate periods from the spacing of matching k-tuples before aligning (Benson, 1999), so the difference is not that the information is unavailable to them. It is that an FM index locates the query in the index in time proportional to the kmer length alone, independent of the size of the genome and of how often the kmer occurs, and then yields the occurrence list at a cost proportional to its length — where a scan pays for the sequence whether or not the kmer is there. That is what allows BWTandem to identify candidate periods before committing to alignment — the step we infer dominates TRF's cost on satellite-dense sequence from the Arabidopsis–maize inversion below, not from profiling.
		 The sharpest cost contrast in these experiments is between the two genomes, and it is about TRF rather than about BWTandem. TRF took 131.9 hours on the 132 Mb Arabidopsis genome and 5.5 hours on the 2.18 Gb maize genome (Table 3C), a genome sixteen times larger, both runs capped at the same maximum period of 200 bp; both cells are inherited cost figures without surviving job records (Section 2.2.1). This is consistent with TRF's cost tracking repeat density rather than genome size, with dense CEN180 arrays the most plausible driver given how concentrated they are in the Arabidopsis assembly, a pattern already noted for TRF on satellite rich sequence generally (Wlodzimierz et al., 2023). BWTandem does not show that inversion — 0.51 hours on Arabidopsis against 15.4 hours on maize, a thirtyfold increase for a sixteenfold size increase, so it is superlinear in genome size on this pair — though the maize run additionally enabled the catch-all pass, which the Arabidopsis run did not, so the contrast is not configuration-clean — and we have no evidence that it is density-insensitive in general. What the pair does establish is that the tool most exposed to satellite density is the alignment-based one, which is the direction an indexing based approach predicts; we have not isolated the effect to kmer seeding specifically, and a two-genome contrast in one tool cannot.
		 4.4 Limitations
		 Three limitations bound the results above. The first is period assignment. Filtering calls by reported period costs BWTandem 15.6, 33.0, and 17.8 percentage points of coverage on knob180, TR-1, and CentC, against at most 1.5 for TRF and 2.8 for ULTRA (Tables 3B-b, 3C-b): it covers these arrays as well as any tool that also places sub-kilobase boundaries (TRASH covers 0.9–2.7 points more, at 10 kb offsets) but frequently assigns a period outside the nominal monomer band, so an analysis selecting calls by expected monomer length will discard correct detections. Whether those periods are harmonics of the true monomer, and whether post-processing could recover the nominal period without re-running detection, remains open; an analogous question at the period-100 boundary in human was tested on the subset for which the test is defined and the harmonic explanation did not hold there, so it should not be assumed here.

		 The second is memory. BWTandem holds one FM-index per in-flight chromosome, so its footprint is the largest of the de novo tools compared on the two large genomes — 21.86 GB on human at two threads against ULTRA's 1.68 GB and tantan's 0.27 GB; on Arabidopsis ULTRA's 4.20 GB exceeds its 1.95 GB — and grows with thread count rather than being fixed: measured on human at the full range, 13.32 GB at one thread and 21.38 GB at two, a fixed base near 5 GB plus roughly 8 GB per thread (Section 2.2.1). It is not the largest figure in these experiments: the template-guided NCRF required 80.96 GB on Arabidopsis, and TRASH in template mode required up to 120.6 GB on maize — its template-mode cost varies with the template across the three maize experiments, 65.5–101.7 h and 12.78–120.64 GB. The distinction matters for a user choosing between them, since BWTandem's requirement is tunable downward to about 13 GB at one thread, still the largest de novo floor on this genome; we did not test whether NCRF's is similarly tunable.

		 The third concerns the regions BWTandem reports that no other tool does — 875,224 calls, 21.9% of its output, measured on the matched-range run of Tables 1b and 1d-F against ULTRA, tantan, TRF and TRASH, and characterised in Supplementary Table S2. They do not carry the signature of low-complexity artefacts such a count usually indicates: their median length is within 1 bp of the calls the other tools share (25 against 24 bp), and their mean motif entropy is higher, 1.24 bits against 1.03, with fewer of them below 1 bit (38.9% against 46.4%). They skew toward longer periods than the shared calls, not shorter: the 21-100 bp band holds 21.9% of them against 6.1% of shared calls, while 13.2% are mononucleotide or dinucleotide against 24.8%. 91% of them lie outside the adotto catalog, a fraction unchanged by whether the periodicity pass is enabled. This benchmark cannot adjudicate them. They are absent from the catalog and from every other tool's output, and some of that absence is by design, since tantan is built for masking and TRF's thresholds exclude short arrays. Establishing whether they are detections or over-calls requires evidence this comparison does not contain, such as orthogonal validation on the same loci or a catalog built without the coverage assumptions adotto makes.
		 4.5 Future directions
		 A compressed occurrence structure, such as a wavelet tree rank index (Grossi et al., 2003), could reduce the occurrence-array term of the footprint without changing the underlying seeding algorithm — though the 32-bit suffix array is the larger term (Supplementary Methods S1.5), and runtime, superlinear in genome size on the one pair measured (Section 4.3), would need separate attention before genomes substantially larger than these three become practical. Beyond implementation, the periodic flanking sequence measured here points at a biological question. On the fourteen of fifty-nine curated maize arrays where BWTandem extends more than 500 bp past the curated coordinates, the extension carries the family period: thirteen of the fourteen exceed the 95th percentile of intergenic background, at a median identity of 0.430 that is above every family's interior median. At those loci the curated boundary looks like a scoring decision rather than a sequence transition. The base is narrow, though, and the observation is largely about one family — eleven of the fourteen flanks are TR-1, and the single CentC flank is the exception at 0.269, indistinguishable from the 0.268 intergenic median and the only one of the fourteen not to clear the 95th percentile of background. Establishing how general that is would require the same measurement across curated satellite catalogs in other species, and would bear on how array boundaries are defined rather than on any one tool. The unique regions absent from the adotto catalog would offer a similarly direct test of whether the human tandem repeat catalog is systematically missing particular repeat classes or genomic contexts, if independently validated — the characterisation here is enough to design that validation, not to run it: longer periods than the corroborated calls, higher motif diversity, and 91% outside the catalog. Extending the approach to noisy long reads, as TideHunter does (Gao et al., 2019), would carry the same recall favoring logic into repeat genotyping ahead of assembly, which is a different setting from annotation of an already finished genome.
		 A tool built to be useful before a curated repeat catalog exists is answering a different question than a tool built to refine calls within one. If T2T assemblies continue to accumulate across non model species faster than repeat libraries can be built for them — which we expect but have not quantified — that distinction will matter more than which tool reports the tightest boundary on any single benchmark.

		 Supplementary Methods
		 S1. BWTandem Parameter Specifications
		 S1.1 Tier 1: Short Tandem Repeat Detection
		 A Tier 1 candidate of period p and length L base pairs is retained when both of the following hold.

		 L ≥ max(L_min, p · C_min(p))     and     Q = L · (1 − e) ≥ Q_min

		 Here e is the mismatch rate, Q is the quality score used throughout the pipeline, and C_min is a period-dependent minimum copy count.

		 C_min(p) = max(2, ⌊6/p⌋ + 2)

		 This gives 8 copies for mononucleotide repeats, 5 for dinucleotides, 4 for trinucleotides, 3 for periods of 4 to 6 bp, and 2 for 7 to 9 bp. The implementation's built-in defaults are max(3, ⌊12/p⌋ + 3); the values above are the settings used for every run reported in this paper, applied through the TIER1_COPYBASE, TIER1_COPYADD and TIER1_MIN_COPIES environment overrides.

		 The length floor L_min and score floor Q_min are 17 for the runs using the relaxed short-period gate and 20 for the BWTandem-P configuration, which omits it. The copy-count term is therefore not the binding constraint for most periods: at L_min = 17, a mononucleotide array must reach 17 copies rather than 8, a dinucleotide 9 rather than 5, and a trinucleotide 6 rather than 4. The copy count binds at p = 6 and p = 9, where p · C_min(p) = 18 exceeds the 17 bp floor; under BWTandem-P's floor of 20 it never binds. A reader tuning sensitivity should treat the length and score floors as the primary levers, not the copy-count formula.

		 In the configuration used throughout this paper the FM-index enumeration runs as a second, additive pass: the sliding-window scanner runs first under the gate above, and the enumeration then scans only the residual sequence that pass did not already claim, so it contributes the diverged short arrays an exact-seed scan cannot reach without double-reporting the ones it can. It enumerates every primitive motif rotation of length 1 to 6 bp, rotations not being collapsed to a canonical form, and applies its own acceptance gate rather than the one above: at least three occurrences of the motif, a span of at least 20 bp, gaps of at most two motif copies within a progression, a log-likelihood ratio for periodic hit testing of 6.0 against an independent-bases null whose base frequencies are estimated from a subsample of up to two million bases, a minimum density of 0.45, and a per-motif cap of 2×10⁷ genome-wide occurrences, above which the motif is skipped.
		 S1.2 Tier 2: Medium-Length Repeat Detection
		 In the LCP-driven phase, the detected motif is reduced to its primitive period using exact divisibility checks and approximate periodicity tests with a tolerance of 2%, implemented via autocorrelation.

		 In the BWT k-mer seeding phase, the k-mer and stride formulas resolve to 9 bp k-mers sampled every 5 bp for the 10–50 bp scan and 10 bp k-mers every 10 bp for the long-unit phase, and progressions are accepted within a 3% jitter tolerance. The LCP thresholds are 10 for the long-unit phase and 8 for the 10–50 bp scan's LCP pre-pass. The mismatch tolerance for Tier 2 was set to 30% in every run reported here, against a library default of 20%, and the same rate applies to both phases. The seeding phase covers periods of 10–50 bp and the strict LCP phase periods of 20 bp and above.
		 S1.3 Tier 3: Adaptive Parameter Computation and Clamping
		 The GC-content-dependent mismatch rate is computed as follows.

		 m = 0.15 + 0.10 · |GC − 0.5|

		 The GC term is bounded in practice by genomic composition: |GC − 0.5| reaches 0.5 only at 0% or 100% GC, so the formula's nominal 20% ceiling is unreachable on real sequence. GC is measured per chromosome over the assembled sequence including N runs; evaluated at whole-genome GC the formula gives 0.159 for human, 0.164 for Arabidopsis and 0.153 for maize, and the per-chromosome values in force vary around those with each chromosome's composition.

		 For sequences exceeding 100 Mb, a large-chromosome mode caps the maximum k-mer occurrence count at 500 and imposes a minimum stride of 150 and a minimum k-mer size of 20. These caps are applied before clamping. The parameters are computed per chromosome, and the 100 Mb gate divides the chromosomes analysed here: the occurrence cap of 500 binds only above 100 Mb (every maize chromosome and the larger human ones), while chromosomes below it take the formula value clamped to 1,500 — roughly 730–1,090 on the five Arabidopsis chromosomes and the full 1,500 on mid-sized human chromosomes — so a 90 Mb chromosome receives three times the occurrence budget of a 250 Mb one. The stride and k-mer minima never bind, because the base formulas already exceed them at this scale.

		 All adaptive parameters are then clamped to empirically validated ranges. The rightmost column gives the values in force across the chromosomes of the three genomes benchmarked here; where a range is shown, the realized value depends on chromosome length.

| Parameter | Minimum | Maximum | Values in force here |
|---|--:|--:|--:|
| K-mer size | 12 | 28 | 24–28 (24–25 Arabidopsis, 26–28 human, 28 maize) |
| Sampling stride | 20 | 300 | 300 |
| Max k-mer occurrences | 200 | 1,500 | 500 above 100 Mb; ~730–1,500 below |
| Mismatch rate | 15% | 20% | 15.3–16.4% |
| Anchor scan, backward units | 20 | 80 | 20–80 |
| Anchor scan, forward units | 200 | 800 | 200–800 |

			 The Tier-3 period jitter tolerance clamps to its 0.04 ceiling in every run, since the tier's internal maximum period is 100 kb.

		 One consequence is that the coverage-dependent stride reduction described in Section 2.1.4 is inert at chromosome scale: halving a base stride of n / 40,000 still exceeds the 300 ceiling for any sequence above roughly 24 Mb, so the clamp binds first and the stride is 300 regardless of prior tier coverage.

		 The anchor-based boundary verification threshold for large arrays ranges from 70% to 80% match fraction, as determined by the adaptive parameter computation. For smaller arrays, banded Needleman-Wunsch alignment is applied copy by copy in the 5′-to-3′ direction against a consensus that is updated after each accepted copy, so the procedure is a rolling consensus rather than an independent alignment per copy and its result depends on copy order. Copies are aligned with an indel allowance of max(1, ⌈p/10⌉) and a band half-width of that allowance plus two, so total cost is proportional to copies × p × (⌈p/10⌉ + 2).
		 S1.4 Post-Processing: Autocorrelation Identity
		 The autocorrelation identity at period p over a window W of length w is defined as follows.

		 A(W, p) = (1 / (w − p)) · Σᵢ₌₀^{w−p−1} 𝟙[W[i] = W[i + p]]

		 where 𝟙 denotes the indicator function. For satellite gap-filling, the default minimum identity threshold is 45%; only uncovered blocks of 300 bp to 100 kb within 50 kb of an anchoring call are examined, identity is sampled from at most three 5 kb windows within the block with an early exit once one reaches 0.80, and the accepted block is emitted whole. For the catch-all periodicity sweep, windows of twice the candidate period length, subject to a 12 bp floor, are evaluated using a cumulative sum technique, enabling O(n) computation across all positions; accepted stretches must span at least 20 bp, are reported out to the end of the last passing window, and are discarded if more than 30% of the span is already covered by an existing call.
		 S1.5 Index Storage
		 The suffix array is stored as 32-bit integers and the BWT as an 8-bit character array. Occurrence counts are maintained through checkpointed arrays sampled every 128 positions and stored as 32-bit integers per alphabet symbol — roughly 0.2 bytes per base against the suffix array's four — enabling rank queries with constant-time checkpoint lookups followed by short linear scans.
